# Capital Structure

## 1. The foundation: every company runs on other people's money

A company holds things. It holds factories, stock, trucks, patents and cash. We call these things **assets**.

Somebody paid for those assets. There are only two kinds of somebody:

- **Lenders.** They give money and want it back, plus interest. Their claim is **debt**.
- **Owners.** They give money and want a share of the profit. Their claim is **equity**.

This gives you the one equation that everything else sits on:

**Assets = Debt + Equity**

**Capital structure** is the mix. It is the answer to one question: how much of this company is funded by lenders, and how much by owners?

That is the whole idea. Everything below is detail on top of it.

## 2. The funding menu is longer than two items

**Debt (you must pay it back):**

1. A bank loan with a fixed term.
2. A revolving credit line. The company draws money, repays it, and draws again.
3. Bonds and notes. The company borrows from many investors in the market instead of one bank.
4. Leases. The company rents a plane, a shop or a machine. Accounting rules now show most leases as debt on the balance sheet.
5. Trade credit. The supplier delivers goods now and accepts payment in 60 days. This is free debt.
6. Customer advances. The customer pays before delivery. This is also free debt.
7. Factoring. The company sells its unpaid customer invoices for cash today.

**Equity (you do not pay it back):**

8. Retained earnings. The company keeps its own profit instead of paying it out. This is the biggest source of funding for most mature companies, and people forget it.
9. New ordinary shares.
10. Private equity or venture capital money.

**Hybrids (they sit in the middle):**

11. Preference shares. They pay a fixed dividend like debt, but they rank below debt.
12. Convertible bonds. They start as debt and can turn into shares later.

So debt is normal. Almost every company uses at least trade credit. The real question is how much and what kind.

## 3. Why companies borrow at all

There are three reasons, and they build on each other.

**Reason one: debt is cheaper than equity.** A lender takes less risk than an owner. The lender gets paid first, and often holds security over an asset. Less risk means the lender accepts a lower return. An owner takes all the leftover risk, so an owner demands more.

**Reason two: the tax shield.** A company pays interest before it pays tax. So interest cuts the tax bill. Dividends do not. If a company pays 100 in interest and the tax rate is 25, then the real cost is only 75. Governments quietly subsidise borrowing.

**Reason three: debt does not dilute the owners.** If you issue new shares, then the existing owners hold a smaller slice. If you borrow, then they keep their full slice.

**Leverage** is the word for the effect of debt on owner returns. Here is the whole idea in numbers.

A company has assets of 100 and makes a profit of 10 before financing.

- With no debt: the owners put in 100 and earn 10. The return on equity is 10%.
- With 50 debt at 6% interest: the owners put in 50. Interest costs 3. The owners earn 7 on 50. The return on equity is 14%.

Debt made the owners richer. That is why finance directors like it.

## 4. The other side: leverage cuts both ways

Now the same company has a bad year. The profit before financing falls to 4.

- With no debt: the owners earn 4 on 100. The return is 4%. It is a poor year, but nothing breaks.
- With 50 debt: interest is still 3. The owners earn 1 on 50. The return is 2%.

Now make it worse. The profit falls to 2.

- With no debt: the owners earn 2%. The company survives.
- With 50 debt: interest is 3 and profit is 2. The company cannot pay. The bank can seize assets or force a sale.

This is the key point of the whole tutorial. **Debt does not change the average outcome. It widens the range of outcomes.** The good years get better and the bad years get fatal. A loan creates a *fixed cost* and, as a result, makes the company more *leveraged*.

## 5. So how much debt is too much?

There is no single number. The honest answer is a rule, not a number.

**A company can carry as much debt as its worst likely year can service.**

Not its best year. Not its average year. Its bad year. So the safe level of debt depends on how steady the cash flow is.

Two companies with the same profit can have very different safe limits:

- A water utility sells water. Demand does not fall in a recession. Its cash flow next year looks like this year. It can carry a lot of debt.
- A steel maker sells into a cycle. Its profit can fall by 80% in two years. It must carry much less debt.

The academic view says the same thing. Debt gives a tax benefit, and that benefit grows with more debt. Debt also raises the chance of financial distress, and that cost grows faster with more debt. The best level sits where the two effects balance. In practice, nobody can measure that point exactly, so managers use industry norms and the opinion of the rating agencies.

## 6. When borrowing is a good idea

Look for these signs. They usually appear together.

1. **The money earns more than it costs.** If the new factory returns 12% and the debt costs 6% after tax, then borrowing creates value. This is the single most important test.
2. **The cash flow is steady and easy to predict.** Subscriptions, regulated tariffs and long contracts support debt well.
3. **The assets are hard and easy to sell.** Property, ships and aircraft make good security. A lender lends more, and lends cheaper, against them.
4. **The rate is fixed and the term is long.** A company that fixed its rate for ten years in a cheap year holds a real advantage.
5. **The debt funds growth, and not survival.** Borrowing to build capacity is different from borrowing to pay wages.
6. **The maturity matches the asset.** A 20 year power plant should be funded with long debt. Funding it with 12 month loans is a trap.

## 7. When borrowing is a bad idea and signals trouble

These are the warning signs. Any one of them deserves attention. Two or more together are serious.

1. **The company borrows to pay interest on old debt.** This is the clearest danger sign there is.
2. **The company borrows to pay a dividend or to buy back its own shares.** It is moving money from lenders to owners, and it is not building anything.
3. **The maturities do not match.** The company funds a long term asset with short term loans. It must refinance often, and it can fail at the worst moment.
4. **Debt grows faster than earnings.** Track debt and EBITDA for five years. If the ratio climbs every year, then the trend matters more than the level.
5. **A large amount of debt matures in one single year.** People call this a maturity wall. The company must refinance in whatever market exists on that date.
6. **The debt is at a floating rate in a rising rate world.** The interest cost can double while sales stay flat.
7. **The debt is in a foreign currency and the income is not.** A fall in the local currency raises the debt without raising the income.
8. **The company is close to breaching a covenant.** A covenant is a promise in the loan agreement. A common one says that net debt must stay below 3.5 times EBITDA. If the company breaks it, then the lender can demand the money back at once. This turns a bad year into a crisis.

## 8. Is issuing new shares always a sign of trouble?

No. It depends on who is issuing and why.

**Normal and healthy reasons to issue shares:**

- The company is young and grows fast. It has no profit and no assets to pledge. Equity is its only real option. This is normal for early software and biotech companies.
- The company buys another company and pays with its own shares.
- The company funds a big project that is too risky for a lender.
- The company repairs its balance sheet after a one time shock, and it does so early and from strength.

**Reasons that should worry you:**

- The company issues shares again and again, each time at a lower price.
- The money goes on wages and interest, and not on assets or growth.
- The shares are sold at a deep discount to the market price.
- The share count grows every year, but the profit does not. Each old owner then holds less of the same pie.

There is a useful idea here called **signalling**. Managers know more about their company than you do. They tend to issue shares when they think the shares are expensive, and they tend to borrow when they think the shares are cheap. So the market often marks the price down on the news of a share issue. That reaction is a suspicion, and not a verdict.

This leads to the **pecking order**. Most managers fund a company in this order:

1. Retained profit first, because it is easy and it signals nothing.
2. Debt second, because it is cheap and it does not dilute owners.
3. New shares last, because the market reads it as bad news.

If a healthy company skips to step three, then ask why. If a loss making growth company goes straight to step three, then that is simply how its industry works.

## 9. The ratios you need

Sort them into four groups. Each group answers a different question.

### Group A: how much debt is there?

- **Debt to equity** = total debt divided by shareholders' equity. It is the classic measure. It is easy to distort, because equity is an accounting number and can be small or even negative after buybacks.
- **Net debt** = total debt minus cash. Cash can repay debt, so subtract it.
- **Net debt to EBITDA** is the most used measure in the market. EBITDA is a rough proxy for cash profit before interest, tax and the accounting charges for wear and tear. The ratio answers a simple question: how many years of cash profit would it take to repay the debt? Below 2 is comfortable for most industries. Above 4 needs a good reason. Above 6 is high risk outside of utilities and property.
- **Debt to total assets** shows the share of the company that lenders funded.

### Group B: can the company pay?

This group matters more than Group A. Companies fail when they cannot pay, and not when a ratio looks ugly.

- **Interest cover** = EBIT divided by interest expense. EBIT is profit before interest and tax. Above 5 is comfortable. Below 2 is tight. Below 1 means the profit does not cover the interest.
- **Free cash flow to net debt.** Free cash flow is the cash left after the company pays for its own upkeep and growth. This is the real repayment capacity.
- **Debt service cover** = cash available divided by the interest plus the repayments due this year. Lenders care about this one most.

### Group C: can the company survive the next 12 months?

- **Current ratio** = current assets divided by current liabilities. Around 1.5 is normal.
- **Quick ratio** removes stock from current assets, because stock can be slow to sell.

### Group D: does the debt create value?

- **WACC** is the weighted average cost of capital. It blends the cost of debt and the cost of equity into one number. It is the minimum return the company must earn.
- **ROIC** is the return on invested capital. It is the return the company actually earns on all the money in the business.
- **The master test: ROIC must beat WACC.** If it does, then the company creates value, and more capital is good. If it does not, then more capital destroys value, and more debt only speeds up the damage.

One warning about return on equity. Debt lifts return on equity even when the business does not improve. A high return on equity with a high debt load is not the same as a high return on equity with no debt. Check the debt before you admire the return.

## 10. Where to find the numbers

- **The annual report.** In the United States this is the 10-K. Read the balance sheet, then read the notes. The debt note is the treasure. It lists every loan, the interest rate, the repayment date and the covenants.
- **The maturity table** in that note shows how much debt falls due in each of the next five years. This finds the maturity wall.
- **The quarterly report,** which is the 10-Q in the United States.
- **The management discussion section.** It has a part on liquidity and capital resources. Management describes its own funding plan there.
- **The cash flow statement.** The financing section shows exactly how much debt the company raised and repaid, and how many shares it issued.
- **Free sources:** SEC EDGAR, the company investor relations page, stockanalysis.com, and the major finance portals.
- **Credit rating reports** from the big agencies. They explain the reasoning behind a rating in plain language.

One caution. Free websites calculate the same ratio in different ways. Some include lease liabilities in debt, and some do not. Some include pension deficits. Check the definition before you compare two companies.

## 11. Industries are not the same, and you must read them differently

This is the part that most beginners get wrong. A debt to equity ratio of 3 is dangerous in one industry and conservative in another.

- **Utilities, pipelines and toll roads.** The cash flow is regulated and steady. Net debt to EBITDA of 4 to 6 is normal and safe. Low debt here can even mean lazy management.
- **Property and real estate trusts.** Debt is the product. Use loan to value and interest cover instead of debt to equity. The value of the property matters more than the profit.
- **Banks and insurers.** Do not use these ratios at all. A bank borrows money as its raw material. Its debt to equity is often above 10, and that is normal. Use capital adequacy measures instead, such as the CET1 ratio.
- **Telecom and cable.** The networks cost a lot and the subscription income is steady. Net debt to EBITDA of 2.5 to 3.5 is standard.
- **Consumer staples.** Food and household goods sell in every economy. Moderate debt is safe.
- **Software and internet.** There are few hard assets to pledge and the growth is fast. Many of these companies hold more cash than debt. Frequent share issues are normal, and much of it is pay for staff.
- **Biotechnology before approval.** There is no revenue at all. The company is pure equity, and it must issue shares often. That is the business model, and it is not a warning sign by itself.
- **Airlines, shipping, autos and steel.** These industries combine heavy assets with a hard cycle. That mix is the most dangerous one in finance. Judge their debt against a bad year, and not against the last good one.
- **Mining, oil and gas.** The profit follows a commodity price that they do not control. Test the debt against a mid cycle price, and not the current price.
- **Retail.** The lease liabilities are large. Old accounts hid them off the balance sheet. Always add leases to debt here.
- **Construction and engineering.** Customers pay in advance, so the working capital can fund the business. Cash on the balance sheet can belong to projects, and not to the company.

The practical rule is short. Compare a company with its own industry, and with its own history. Never compare it with the whole market.

## 12. Extra points that experienced readers check

1. **Hidden debt.** Look for lease liabilities, pension deficits, factored receivables, guarantees given to other parties, and debt inside joint ventures.
2. **Trapped cash.** Cash held in a foreign subsidiary may not be free to move. Cash also has a minimum working level. Net debt can flatter a company.
3. **The rating boundary.** Rating agencies split the world at BBB minus. Above it is investment grade. Below it is high yield, and the borrowing cost jumps. Many companies manage their debt to stay just above that line.
4. **Fixed against floating.** Find the split in the debt note. It tells you how a rate change hits the company.
5. **The theory in one line.** Modigliani and Miller showed that in a perfect world with no taxes and no bankruptcy, the capital structure would not matter at all. The real world has taxes and bankruptcy. That is exactly why it does matter.

## 13. A ten minute check on any company

1. Read the debt note. Find total debt, cash and net debt.
2. Divide net debt by EBITDA. Compare it with the industry norm above.
3. Divide EBIT by interest expense. If it is below 3, then slow down and look closer.
4. Read the maturity table. Ask what happens if the company cannot refinance next year.
5. Look at the five year trend in the share count and in net debt.
6. Compare ROIC with WACC.
7. Read the covenant terms and measure the headroom.

If the trend is stable, the cover is wide and the returns beat the cost of capital, then the debt is a tool. If the trend is worse each year and the cover is thin, then the debt is a countdown.
