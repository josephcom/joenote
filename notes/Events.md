# Corporate Events: A Plain-English Guide

*Six special situations, what actually moves the price, and what to do about it.*

---

## How to read this

Before the list, one thing that makes all of this click: share prices move on three different kinds of reason, and only the first two are "real."

1. **Arithmetic** — your slice of the future cash is now bigger, or a liability just vanished.
2. **Plumbing** — someone is *forced* to buy or sell, regardless of value.
3. **Story** — the market decides to feel differently about the same facts.

Most event-driven moves are 1 and 2. Now each event.

---

## 1. Merger / takeover

Company A offers to buy Company B. Almost always at a **premium** — typically 20–40% above where B was trading yesterday. B's shareholders get cash, or A's shares, or a mix.

**Why B's price jumps:** pure arithmetic. There is now a named buyer, with a signed agreement, willing to pay a specific number. If the offer is $50 and the stock trades at $45, that $5 gap isn't sentiment — it's the market's estimate of two things: the chance the deal collapses, and the time you'll wait for your money.

Why does a buyer pay above market at all? Control is worth more than a passive stake. They expect to cut duplicate costs, or they want the customers, patents, or licences. And they must offer enough to persuade a majority of owners to actually let go.

**The trade** (called merger arbitrage): buy B after the announcement, wait, pocket the gap. Sounds like free money. It isn't — that gap is an **insurance premium** you're being paid for absorbing deal risk. Which is precisely why the payoff is lopsided. You capture the last 4%; if the deal dies, the stock instantly falls back to $35 and you lose 25%.

**Deals die because:** the competition regulator blocks it, a foreign-investment body vetoes it, the acquirer's financing evaporates, shareholders vote no, due diligence uncovers something ugly, or the buyer simply gets cold feet and pays a break fee. You cannot see most of these coming from the outside.

One more thing: A's own stock usually *falls* on the announcement. Paying a premium and taking on debt is a real cost, and the market is sceptical of promised savings.

### Example

Small's shares trade at **$10**. Big offers **$14** each. Next morning Small jumps to **$13**.

Why not $13 → $14? Because the deal might fail. If it completes, you get $14 — you made **$1**. If a regulator blocks it, the price falls straight back to **$10** — you lost **$3**.

Risk $3 to make $1. That's the whole game.

**Why they do it:** Big wants Small's customers and can cut duplicate costs. Control is worth more than a small stake, so Big pays extra to win owners over.

**You should** decide between selling now at $13 for certainty, or holding for $14 and accepting the risk of falling back to $10.

**No shares:** You could buy at $13 hoping for $14. Small gain, big risk — only sensible if you've read the deal terms yourself.

## 2. Spin-off

A company carves a division out into a separate listed company. You keep your original shares and are handed shares in the new one, in proportion.

Note that day one isn't magic wealth. The parent's share price drops by roughly the value of what walked out the door. So why do spin-offs tend to do well afterwards? Three genuine reasons:

**The conglomerate discount is real.** A company that's part mining, part software, part logistics gets valued as a confused mess. Analysts who cover mining won't touch it; software investors won't own a mining exposure. Split it up and each piece gets valued by the people who understand it — often on a much higher multiple. Same assets, higher combined price. Arithmetic, not mood.

**Incentives change.** The small division that was ignored inside a giant now has its own board, its own balance sheet, and management paid on *its* performance instead of the parent's. Capital stops being siphoned to the parent's pet projects.

**Forced selling — this is the big one.** Index funds tracking a large-cap index get handed shares in a small company that doesn't belong in their index. They must sell. A healthcare fund gets handed an industrial business. Must sell. None of them are selling because the business is bad; they're selling because their mandate forbids holding it. That indiscriminate dumping in the first few weeks can push the price well below fair value, and it recovers. This is a **structural** inefficiency, not an emotional one.

**Trap:** parents sometimes use spin-offs as a bin — loading the new entity with debt, pension obligations, or the legal liabilities they want off their own books. You only find that in the documents.

### Example

Farm Co owns an orchard *and* a shoe shop. Share price **$10**.

It splits. You keep 1 Farm share, now **$7**, plus 1 free Shoe share worth **$3**. Still $10 total.

Then funds that only hold farms get handed Shoe shares. They must dump them. Shoe falls to **$2** — even though nothing inside the shoe shop changed. Weeks later it drifts back to $3.

**Why they do it:** Investors discounted the confusing mix. Apart, each business is judged by people who understand it, with its own boss and its own accounts.

**You should** not sell the free Shoe shares alongside everyone else — that's when it's cheapest. Check the booklet for debt dumped into Shoe.

**No shares:** You could wait for the forced selling to finish, then buy Shoe at $2 while nobody's watching.

## 3. Buyback (share repurchase)

The company uses its own cash to buy its own shares on market and cancel them.

**The real mechanism:** fewer slices, same pie. If a company earns $100m with 100m shares, that's $1 per share. Buy back 10m shares and it's $1.11 per share — an 11% lift with zero improvement in the business. Everyone who *didn't* sell now owns a bigger fraction of the company. That's arithmetic.

**Second real mechanism:** the company itself becomes a large, persistent buyer in the market every day. That's genuine demand meeting a fixed supply.

**Where it turns into story:** the announcement itself is treated as a signal — "management thinks we're cheap." Sometimes true. Often not, because companies have the most spare cash at the top of the cycle and none at the bottom, so they systematically buy their own shares expensively and stop buying when they're cheap. Buying back stock above its worth destroys value just as surely as overpaying for an acquisition.

Three things worth checking before believing a buyback:

- **Was it actually done?** An announced buyback is an *authorisation*, not a commitment. Many are never completed.
- **Did the share count actually fall?** Pull up shares outstanding over five years. In many companies the buyback merely mops up shares issued to executives — the count never drops. Nothing was returned to you.
- **Was it funded with debt?** Then you haven't been given value; you've been given leverage.

### Example

Company has **10 shares** and earns **$10** a year — so **$1 per share**. Price **$10**.

It buys back and cancels **2 shares**. Now 8 shares split the same $10 profit = **$1.25 each**. Your share is worth about **$12.50**.

You did nothing. Your slice got bigger.

But if it paid **$15** per share to buy them back, it overpaid — and everyone still holding is worse off.

**Why they do it:** Spare cash, nothing better to build. Shrinking the share count lifts earnings per share — which often lifts management's bonus too.

**You should** check the share count actually fell over five years. If it didn't, the buyback only mopped up shares given to executives.

**No shares:** You could favour companies that quietly shrink their share count yearly at sensible prices. Avoid ones borrowing to do it.

## 4. Rights issue

The company needs money, so it offers **existing** shareholders the right to buy new shares at a discount, in proportion to what they hold — e.g. one new share for every four you own, at 20% below market.

**Important correction to your mental model: this normally pushes the price DOWN, not up.** More shares exist, so each one is worth less. The post-issue price settles at a blended average of the old price and the cheap new price. That's dilution, and it's automatic.

So why is it on an event-driven list? Because the opportunity is in the mechanics, not the direction:

- The rights themselves usually trade separately for a few days and are frequently mispriced.
- Shareholders who can't or won't participate — people on holiday, small holders, funds with no spare cash — sell into a thin market, pushing prices below fair value.
- If you don't take up your rights, you are silently diluted. Doing nothing is a decision that costs money.

**When it genuinely lifts the price:** if the market feared the company might not survive its debt load, a successful raise removes that risk. A collapse of bankruptcy risk is a real re-rating — the range of possible futures just lost its worst branch. Same if the money funds an obviously value-adding purchase.

**Trap:** raising equity is usually a symptom. Healthy companies fund themselves. Also watch who's allowed in — institutional-only placements at a discount transfer value from you to them.

### Example

You own **4 shares at $10** = **$40**.

The company needs cash. It offers you **1 new share at $5** for every 4 you hold. You pay $5. Now you own **5 shares** and have spent **$45** — so each is worth **$9**.

If you *don't* pay: you still own 4 shares, now worth $9 each = **$36**. You lost **$4** by doing nothing.

**Why they do it:** It needs money — to repay debt, survive, or buy something. Existing owners get first offer at a discount so they aren't unfairly diluted.

**You should** either take up the rights or sell them if they're tradeable. Doing nothing is the only option that guarantees a loss.

**No shares:** You could buy discounted rights from holders who can't pay. First ask why the company needs money.

## 5. Index inclusion

A stock gets added to a major index — S&P 500, ASX 200, an MSCI index. Every passive fund tracking that index is now *obliged* to buy it. They have no opinion. Their rules say buy.

**Why the price rises: pure plumbing.** Passive funds hold an enormous share of the market. When they all have to buy a fixed number of shares from a fixed supply, price goes up. It has nothing whatsoever to do with the business — nothing changed inside the company.

But this is the weakest edge on the list, for three reasons:

- Inclusions are announced *before* they take effect, so traders front-run the index funds. The move happens in that window, and by the time the stock is formally in, it's over.
- Everyone knows this now. The "index effect" that was worth several percent decades ago has been arbitraged down to roughly nothing.
- It often fades. Since nothing fundamental changed, the price frequently drifts back.

There are small genuine second-order benefits — more liquidity, broader ownership, slightly cheaper access to capital, more analyst coverage. Modest, and slow.

### Example

A "top 200" list gets updated. Small Co is added.

Funds whose job is to copy that list have no choice — they must buy. Price goes **$10 → $11** in the days beforehand.

Nothing changed. No extra profit, no new customers. Once it joins and the buying stops, it often drifts back to **$10**.

**Why they do it:** Nobody chose it. The rules are mechanical — the company simply grew big enough to qualify, so copycat funds must follow.

**You should** not read the pop as proof your company improved. If you were planning to sell anyway, that window is a decent exit.

**No shares:** The dates are public, so others front-run it. The better-known angle is buying stocks being *dropped* and dumped cheaply.

## 6. Court ruling

A lawsuit, patent dispute, tax case, class action, or regulator's decision.

Before the ruling, the share price already contains a **probability-weighted guess**. If the market thinks there's a 50% chance of a $10bn fine, roughly $5bn of value is already missing from the price. When the ruling lands, that guess collapses into a fact — and the price jumps to close the gap between guess and fact.

**Why this is substantial:** if the fine comes in at $1bn instead of the feared $10bn, $4bn of value returns to the company instantly. That's not relief, it's accounting — a liability that isn't there anymore. Likewise, a patent upheld means years of protected pricing and real future cash.

**The subtler real effect:** uncertainty itself is expensive. Investors apply a heavy discount to risks they can't size, because you can't build a model around "we might owe somewhere between zero and everything." Even a *bad* ruling can lift a share price, simply because a known, quantifiable liability is worth more than an unbounded one.

**Trap:** binary, and the outcome sits with a judge, not a spreadsheet. Appeals drag it out for years. And the people on the other side of your trade include specialist litigation funds with lawyers reading every filing.

### Example

A company faces a lawsuit. It might pay **$6**, or **nothing** — call it a coin flip.

The market takes **$3** off just in case, so it trades at **$7** instead of $10.

The judge rules: pay **$1**. The $6 fear was wrong. Price jumps to about **$9**.

The business didn't change at all. A worry was simply replaced by a fact.

**Why they do it:** They usually don't choose this — it's done to them. They fight because settling early invites more claims, though it drags uncertainty on for years.

**You should** size the worst case in advance. If a $6 hit would hurt badly, trim your holding rather than bet on a judge.

**No shares:** You could buy the fear discount if you've read the case and think $6 is too harsh. Otherwise it's a coin flip.

## So: emotions or substance?

Overwhelmingly substance. Almost every move above traces to something concrete — a contractual price, a share count, a liability erased, a fund legally compelled to trade. The exceptions are the *announcement pops*, where the market rewards the words "unlocking shareholder value" before anyone has checked whether the value exists.

But here's the part that matters more than the classification, and it's what your passage is really warning about.

Because these events are so visible and so easy to reason about, **the obvious money is gone before you can act.** What remains isn't a mispricing — it's residual risk that professionals have deliberately chosen not to hold. Your 4% upside isn't a discovery; it's the fee they're paying you to carry the 30% downside they don't want.

That's a legitimate business. It's just not stock picking — it's insurance underwriting. And underwriters who haven't read the policy go broke, regardless of how sound the reasoning was that got them into the trade.
