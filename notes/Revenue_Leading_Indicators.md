# ASX:GEM (G8 Education) — Revenue Leading Indicators

## The equation

Revenue is centres × licensed places × occupancy × daily fee × operating days, plus government grant income.

Occupancy carries most of the near-term movement. The fee is not fixed by law. G8 sets it, so treat price as a lever G8 chooses, not as a constant.

## How to read this file

- The arrow `→` means "this moves before that".
- A **bold** node is the one you actually watch. It is the earliest point in the chain that is public, free and reliable.
- Chains are numbered so you can point at one in a later conversation.
- There are no point-in-time figures here on purpose. This file is about mechanisms, not readings.

# Market level

## Chains

**M1 — The cash squeeze**
CPI → cash rate → variable mortgage rate → repayment size → **mortgage stress** → savings drawdown → household spending → ability to pay the gap fee

Almost no Australian mortgage is fixed, so this chain runs in weeks, not years. The pain lands on the exact cohort that buys childcare.

**M2 — The reason care is needed at all**
cash rate → **job vacancies** → hiring → unemployment and underemployment → **participation rate** → hours of care a family needs

Vacancies lead employment by about two quarters. Unemployment lags the cash rate by much longer. If you want one early number, use vacancies.

**M3 — Is the second income worth it**
CPI vs the wage index → **real wage growth** → net value of the second income after the daily gap fee → **days booked per child**

This is where the squeeze appears first. A family cuts days before it gives up the place.

**M4 — Wealth and mood**
cash rate → house prices → household wealth → **consumer sentiment** → willingness to take on a repeat cost

A second claim on the same dollar runs in parallel: advertised rents → rental vacancy → rent paid → less money left for the gap fee.

**M5 — Future children**
migration caps → **family-forming arrivals** → future 0–5 population

Headline migration is misleading here. Student visas do not fill childcare rooms. Split the stream and watch the permanent and New Zealand part only.

**M6 — Who pays**
electoral cycle → budget position and stance → **room for subsidy** → subsidy generosity → out-of-pocket fee

Government money funds most of the sector's revenue. This chain sets the price a parent actually feels.

**M7 — Where the work happens**
return-to-office mandates → office days per week → **hours booked per child**

This is currently the only demand chain that points the right way.

## Standalone

- **Credit spreads and small-cap risk appetite.** These change the cost of refinancing and the chance of an equity raise. They do not change revenue.

## Notes

Macro reaches GEM through the sector layer, so treat all of it as second-hand. It also pushes both ways. Higher mortgages and rents make a second income more necessary, and at the same time make the gap fee harder to pay. Childcare is stickier than most discretionary spend on the way down, because a place is hard to get back.

# Sector level

## Chains

**S1 — The feedstock**
cost of living and housing → **births and fertility rate** → the 0–5 population → enrolments

This is the slowest and most reliable chain in the file. Births lead the infant rooms by a year or two, and the preschool rooms by three or four years.

A branch matters: fewer second and third children hurt twice. G8 loses the extra enrolment, and it also loses the higher sibling subsidy rate that comes with it.

**S2 — Who is free to work**
unemployment → **female and parental participation** → demand for care

The old structural tailwind is mostly spent. Participation in the prime parenting cohort is already near its ceiling. Do not model more upside here.

**S3 — The affordability scissor**
budget → **subsidy hourly cap and its yearly indexation** → indexation runs below gross fee growth → **out-of-pocket childcare CPI** → lost enrolments and fewer days

Out-of-pocket childcare inflation runs at roughly double headline inflation. This is the most likely cause of falling enrolments after births.

A branch makes it worse: a large minority of services already charge above the cap. Above the cap, every extra dollar is fully out-of-pocket, with no subsidy offset at all.

**S4 — The cost push behind the fee**
award review and the wage-subsidy program → staged wage rises → provider cost base → **gross fee growth** → out-of-pocket cost (this feeds S3)

The program's fee-growth cap holds fee rises down, but only while a provider takes the grant. When the program ends, fee growth is unconstrained.

**S5 — Competitor supply**
cash rate → building costs and property yields → development feasibility → **development applications** → approvals → openings → service count

The lead is two to four years, and only a minority of applications ever open. Government-funded supply sits alongside this and widens it.

**S6 — The scissor and its self-correction**
service count growth − children-in-care growth → **occupancy drag** → national occupancy falls toward the viability floor → small providers close → supply shrinks → occupancy recovers for the survivors

This is the best single predictor of sector occupancy. Supply grows while demand shrinks, and no current policy closes that gap. Watch the quarterly service count for a second net fall, and the quarterly subsidy data for children in care.

**S7 — Trust**
a child-safety case → national media cycle → **parent trust** → enquiry volume, then conversion → occupancy

This chain is fast and it is not linear. A single case resets trust across the whole country, not only at the operator involved.

**S8 — Regulatory tightening**
child-safety reform → new national law → **enforcement action counts** → power to cancel subsidy approval → services put on notice, surrendered or defunded → supply exits

A branch now touches cash directly. Quality-rating conditions are attached to wage-subsidy funding, so a poor rating cuts money as well as reputation.

**S9 — The future price regime**
price-benchmarking work → a benchmark or a regulated price → **provider pricing power**

This is the biggest open risk in the file. A parallel chain runs the other way: a national commission → supply planning → fewer new entrants → possibly good for incumbents.

**S10 — The age-mix trap**
state preschool programs → funded hours grow in the profitable 3–5 rooms → births fall in the loss-making 0–3 rooms → **room-level margin mix gets worse even if total enrolments hold**

Preschool programs run through long day care, so they are fee substitution and an enrolment magnet, not a competitor. The risk is mix, not competition. A branch adds to it: longer paid parental leave delays entry into the infant room, which is the hardest room to fill and the most staff-heavy.

## Standalone

- **The guaranteed subsidised days reform.** It is real but small, and it has produced no visible enrolment lift. It is too small to close the scissor.
- **Staffing waivers and educator vacancies.** Both are falling. Labour is no longer the binding constraint. Demand is. Useful as a released constraint, not as a predictor.
- **Subsidy income thresholds.** These decide who gets what rate. They move slowly and only at budget time.

# Company level

## Chains

**C1 — The master chain**
sector demand and trust damage → **enquiry volume** → **enquiry-to-enrolment conversion** → spot occupancy → EBIT

Watch the two enquiry metrics separately, because they mean different things. If enquiries hold and conversion falls, then G8 has a price problem. If enquiries themselves fall, then G8 has a trust problem. That shift has already happened, and it is the key qualitative signal in the whole file.

Management publishes a per-point EBIT sensitivity for occupancy. Apply it to the spot reading, not to the reported average.

**C2 — Seasonality**
the preschool cohort leaves for school in late January → February is the annual trough → **the February spot reading is the January intake result** → it sets the revenue base for the rest of the year → October is normally the peak

February effectively sets the year. A failed October lift is the classic trigger for a guidance downgrade. Never compare a February spot with an annual average.

**C3 — Legal news cycles**
charges → each court date → media cycle → parent trust → enquiry volume → occupancy

Several branches run in parallel, and each one is both a cash risk and its own news cycle: criminal charges against the company itself, the workplace regulator's court action, and individual family civil claims. An unscheduled plea or sentencing date is an unpriced catalyst.

**C4 — Network shrinkage**
occupancy → centre-level losses → suspensions → closures → **live centre count on the public services register** → licensed places → revenue capacity

The public register updates continuously and moves ahead of any ASX announcement. It is free, real-time, and the earliest company-specific read available.

Two branches set the speed. Balance-sheet headroom decides how long G8 can carry loss-making centres. Leases decide how expensive it is to leave, because nearly every centre is leased, escalations are fixed, rent is still payable on suspended sites, and landlords are not helping.

**C5 — Price, which is a choice**
the grant's fee-growth cap → G8's self-imposed single yearly January rise → fee revenue

Nothing legally stops a second rise, a bigger rise, or a mid-year re-price. G8 can break the cap and hand back the grant. When the program ends, the cap disappears.

**C6 — Capacity**
capex → greenfield openings → new revenue capacity

No new centres are opening, so no new capacity is coming.

**C7 — Staffing, which reads backwards**
wage rises and weaker sector competition → retention up, vacancies down, time-to-hire down

These improved while occupancy fell hard, so they will not warn you about revenue. What they do tell you is that the demand loss is not a quality problem, and that matters for whether it is recoverable. Agency labour is near zero, so it carries no signal either.

For a real-time read, use job-ad counts by state. They show whether G8 is staffing for recovery or for more shrinkage.

**C8 — Separating GEM from the sector**
GEM occupancy − national occupancy → **the gap** → how much of the damage is company-specific

This is the cleanest single test of whether the problem is G8 or the industry.

## Standalone

- **State and brand mix.** The damage sits in a couple of states. Group numbers hide it.
- **Enforcement actions and licence conditions on the public register.** Watch the state concentration, not the total.
- **Quality ratings profile.** It sits above the sector average, and it is now tied to wage-subsidy eligibility.
- **Net promoter score and employee engagement.** These read backwards, the same as C7. They are not predictive.
- **Substantial holder notices.** A large holder near the takeover threshold turns this into a control question.
- **Board, management and pay outcomes.**

# Cross-cutting notes

**What G8 does not disclose.** The average daily fee, the split between government and parent revenue, the licensed places lost to suspensions, and any liability for the abuse claims. The commonly quoted government share of sector revenue is an industry average, not a company disclosure. Treat any per-place fee figure as your own derivation.

**Definition traps.**
- An annual average occupancy and a February spot are not the same thing. The spot sits at the seasonal trough.
- Sector centre counts differ a lot by source. Long day care centres, subsidy-approved services and all centre-based services are three different populations.
- Headline migration is not family-forming migration.
- A reported half is stale when it lands, because G8 runs a calendar year. The spot occupancy line inside the release is worth more than the half itself.

**What looks like a revenue indicator but is not.** The share price, the small-cap index, credit spreads and the goodwill impairment. These move the multiple and the financing. They do not move the top line.

**If you only watch five things.**
1. The February spot occupancy.
2. The live centre count on the public register.
3. The enquiry volume and conversion split.
4. The supply-minus-demand scissor.
5. Out-of-pocket childcare CPI.
