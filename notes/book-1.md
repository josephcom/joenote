## How to Use This Book

This book uses a building-block approach. Each part assumes only what came before it. Part I explains the bean and the trade. Part II explains the rules. Part III shows you the first deal. Part IV shows the risks. Part V shows how to grow. Part VI holds the tools.

You can follow one of three reading paths. The **Explorer** wants to understand the trade before any commitment. The Explorer reads Parts I–II. The **First-Container Doer** wants to ship one container. The Doer reads Parts I–IV. The **Scaler** wants to build a business. The Scaler reads all parts.

The book uses four symbols. A ✅ marks a checklist. A 💰 marks a cost box or a worked example with numbers. A ⚠️ marks a pitfall warning. A 📖 marks a war story from real trade.

⚠️ **All numbers in this book are toy numbers.** They exist for learning only. They are not current market prices. Real prices move every week. Check real prices before you spend one dollar.

The worked examples follow one recurring character. His name is **Sam**. Sam is a beginner in Brisbane. He has savings, family in Iran, and friends in China. His numbers connect from chapter to chapter. When you see a 💰 Example, it is usually Sam's deal.

One more promise. This book uses one word for one meaning. The Chinese company that buys the beans is always the **buyer**. The Australian farmer is always the **grower**. The facility that cleans and bags beans is always the **packer**. When a technical term first appears, the book defines it in plain words. Every defined term also appears in the glossary in [Appendix A](#appa).

## Chapter 1 — Meet the Mung Bean

<a id="ch01-1"></a>

### 1.1 What ماش actually is: varieties, grades, and the growing cycle

The mung bean is a small green legume. A **legume** is a plant that grows its seeds in pods, like peas. In Farsi, the mung bean is called ماش (*maash*). In Chinese, it is 绿豆 (*lǜdòu*), which means "green bean". People eat the whole bean, its sprouts, or its starch.

Australia grows several varieties. A **variety** is a named type of the plant with known traits. Common Australian varieties include Jade-AU, Crystal, and Celera II-AU. Buyers know these names. Jade-AU is popular because it gives large, shiny, green beans. Large shiny beans earn higher prices.

Traders sort beans into grades. A **grade** is a quality level with defined limits. The Australian industry uses names like Number 1 grade, Processing grade, and Manufacturing grade. Number 1 grade is the best. It has large, even, bright beans with few defects. Lower grades have more cracked, stained, or small beans. Each grade has its own price.

The growing cycle is short. Growers plant mung beans in spring and summer, mostly from September to February. The plant matures in about 90 days. Harvest runs mostly from December to May. This timing matters for trade. It puts fresh Australian beans into the market when some other countries have old stock.

<a id="ch01-2"></a>

### 1.2 What China does with it: bean sprouts, vermicelli (fensi), desserts, flour

China is the biggest user of mung beans in the world. The first big use is bean sprouts. A **bean sprout** is the young shoot that grows from a soaked bean. Sprout factories soak tonnes of beans every day. They need beans that germinate well. **Germination** means the bean wakes up and grows. Dead beans make no sprouts and no money.

The second big use is vermicelli. **Vermicelli** is a thin, glass-like noodle. In Chinese it is called 粉丝 (*fensi*). Factories extract starch from the bean and form it into noodles. These factories care about starch content more than looks.

The third use is food and dessert. Cooks make mung bean soup, a summer drink that Chinese people believe cools the body. Bakers use sweet mung bean paste in mooncakes and pastries. The fourth use is flour and snacks. Mills grind beans into flour for crackers and noodles.

Each use wants a different bean. Sprout makers pay the most and demand the most. They want high germination and zero chemical residue. Noodle and paste makers accept lower grades. Match your bean to the use before you quote a price.

<a id="ch01-3"></a>

### 1.3 The quality language: size, colour, germination rate, moisture, defects

Buyers and sellers describe quality with a few key numbers. Learn them, because contracts use them. First is size. Traders measure size as weight per 100 seeds, or as beans that stay on a test sieve. Bigger beans usually earn more.

Second is colour. Buyers want a bright, even green. Dull or mixed colour lowers the price. Third is **germination rate**. This is the percentage of beans that sprout in a standard test. Sprouting buyers often ask for a rate above about 95%. A lab measures it from a sample.

Fourth is **moisture content**. This is the percentage of water inside the bean. Contracts often set a limit near 14%. Wet beans grow mould and rot inside a sealed container. Fifth is **defects**. Defects include split beans, stained beans, insect-damaged beans, stones, and other seeds. Contracts set a maximum percentage for each defect type.

One more term matters: **foreign material**. This means anything that is not a mung bean, such as soil, sticks, or other grains. Good packers clean beans to very low foreign material levels. Put every one of these numbers in writing in every contract. [Chapter 8](#ch08) shows how.

<a id="ch01-4"></a>

### 1.4 The residue issue: glyphosate, MRLs, and why one number can kill a deal

Now learn the most dangerous word in this trade: residue. A **residue** is a tiny leftover trace of a farm chemical inside the bean. Labs can detect traces at parts per billion. **Glyphosate** is a common weed killer. Some growers spray it late in the season to dry the crop evenly before harvest. That practice can leave residue in the seed.

Every importing country sets MRLs. An **MRL** (maximum residue limit) is the highest legal amount of a chemical residue in food. China sets its own MRLs, and some are stricter than Australia's. For glyphosate on mung beans, China's limit is very low. A crop can be legal in Australia and illegal in China at the same time.

Here is why one number can kill a deal. Chinese customs can sample your container and test it. If one residue number is above the Chinese MRL, customs can reject the whole container. You then pay for return shipping or destruction. You may also lose the buyer. One lab number can erase a year of work.

The defence is simple and cheap. Ask the grower how the crop was finished. Prefer crops that were cut and dried mechanically, not sprayed. Demand a residue test before you buy, and test again before you ship. [Section 10.5](#ch10-5) explains testing. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: China's MRL standard GB 2763 via GACC (customs.gov.cn) and Australian rules via DAFF (agriculture.gov.au).

## Chapter 2 — The World Mung Bean Map

<a id="ch02-1"></a>

### 2.1 The big producers: Myanmar, India, China itself, Australia, Uzbekistan

Five names dominate world mung bean supply. Myanmar is the export giant. It grows large volumes at low cost and sits close to China by land and sea. India grows huge volumes too, but eats most of them at home. India also blocks or taxes exports at times to protect its own food supply.

China grows its own mung beans, mainly in the north-east and Inner Mongolia. But Chinese demand is larger than Chinese supply. The gap is what importers fill. That gap is your market.

Australia is a small producer by volume. Its crop is tiny next to Myanmar or India. Uzbekistan and some other Central Asian countries have grown fast as suppliers in recent years. They sell at prices between Myanmar and Australia. Other origins, such as Kenya and Indonesia, appear in some years.

Do not memorise exact tonnages. They change with every season and drought. Learn the shape instead: Myanmar sells cheap and big, India stays home, China buys the gap, and Australia sells small and premium. For current figures, check ABARES, the Australian government's farm forecaster (agriculture.gov.au/abares).

<a id="ch02-2"></a>

### 2.2 Where Australia fits: the clean, premium, counter-season supplier

Australia cannot win on price. Australian land, labour, and freight cost more than Myanmar's. So Australia wins on three other things. First, cleanliness. Australian farms keep good spray records, and Australian packers clean beans to high standards. Buyers trust the residue story more.

Second, quality. Australian varieties like Jade-AU produce the large, bright, sprouting-grade beans that top buyers want. **Sprouting-grade** means beans good enough for sprout factories, with high germination. This is the premium end of the market.

Third, timing. Australia harvests from about December to May. That is the opposite season to the northern hemisphere. **Counter-season** means your fresh crop arrives when the other side of the world has old stock. Fresh beans germinate better than year-old beans. Sprout factories notice.

So your pitch as an Australian trader is never "cheapest". Your pitch is "clean, premium, fresh at the right time". Buyers who want cheap will buy from Myanmar. Let them. Your buyers are the ones who lose money when germination fails or residue tests fail.

<a id="ch02-3"></a>

### 2.3 China's demand: how much it buys, when, and what it pays

China consumes a very large amount of mung beans every year. It imports a meaningful share of that amount. Exact import numbers move with harvests, prices, and policy. As a rough shape, think in hundreds of thousands of tonnes of imports per year, not millions. Australia supplies only a small slice of that.

Demand has a rhythm. Mung bean soup season peaks in the hot months, so factories stock up before summer. Mooncake season peaks before the Mid-Autumn Festival, around September. Sprout demand runs all year, because people eat sprouts daily. Buyers also stock up before Chinese New Year, when factories close.

Price follows quality and origin. Myanmar beans set the cheap benchmark. Australian sprouting-grade beans earn a premium above that benchmark. The premium moves with supply. In a drought year in Asia, premiums widen. In a glut year, they shrink and Australian beans struggle.

Track prices from real sources, not guesses. Ask packers for their current export quotes. Ask buyers what they pay for each origin. Read market reports from Grain Trade Australia members and commodity news services. Never build a business plan on one price from one conversation.

<a id="ch02-4"></a>

### 2.4 📖 Case study: the 10 rejected containers — anatomy of a failed shipment

📖 Here is a composite war story. The people are fictional. The failure pattern is real, and Australian exporters have lived versions of it. A mid-size trader shipped 10 containers of mung beans to a Chinese port. The trader had a residue test from the grower, taken months earlier at harvest.

Chinese customs sampled the containers. The lab found glyphosate above China's MRL. All 10 containers were rejected. The trader's choices were ugly: ship the beans back, sell them at a loss to another country, or pay for destruction. Every option cost money. The buyer cancelled future orders, because the buyer had lost face with its own customers. **Losing face** means public embarrassment that damages a relationship.

Where did it go wrong? First, the trader tested once, early, and never again. Beans from different paddocks were blended later at the packer. The blend was never tested. Second, the contract did not say clearly who carried the risk of a Chinese test failure. The trader carried it by default. Third, the trader had no insurance for rejection, because standard cargo insurance covers damage, not residue.

The lessons preview the rest of this book. Test the actual export lot, not just the paddock ([Section 10.5](#ch10-5)). Put risk in the contract ([Chapter 8](#ch08)). Know your options after rejection ([Chapter 14](#ch14)). One shipment can fail. Your system must not.

## Chapter 3 — How Export Trade Actually Works

<a id="ch03-1"></a>

### 3.1 One container's journey: paddock → packer → port → vessel → Chinese customs → buyer

Follow one container from start to finish. The journey begins in a paddock. A **paddock** is an Australian word for a farm field. The grower harvests the beans and stores them in a silo on the farm or delivers them to a depot.

Next stop is the packer. The **packer** runs a facility that cleans, grades, and bags the beans. Machines remove dust, stones, split beans, and other seeds. The packer fills 25-kilogram bags or large one-tonne bags, then loads them into a container. A **20-foot container** is a standard steel box, about 6 metres long. It holds roughly 20 to 25 tonnes of bagged beans.

A truck moves the container to the port, such as the Port of Brisbane. The container waits in the terminal, then a crane lifts it onto a vessel. A **vessel** is the ship. The voyage to a southern Chinese port such as Guangzhou's Nansha port takes roughly two to three weeks.

In China, the container meets customs. Officers check documents, and may inspect and test the beans. If all is clear, the buyer's truck collects the container. The beans go to a sprout factory, a noodle factory, or a wholesale market. Money should flow back to you along the way. The rest of this chapter explains the people, the terms, the money, and the paper behind that journey.

<a id="ch03-2"></a>

### 3.2 The cast of characters: grower, agent, packer, exporter, freight forwarder, customs broker, importer, distributor

Eight roles appear in almost every deal. The **grower** farms the beans. The **agent** is a middleman who connects growers with buyers of grain and earns a commission. A **commission** is a fee that is a percentage of the deal.

The **packer** cleans, grades, and bags the beans, as you saw. Many packers also export under their own name. The **exporter** is the business that sells the goods out of Australia and signs the export documents. In this book, that is you.

The **freight forwarder** is your travel agent for cargo. The forwarder books space on vessels, arranges trucks, and prepares shipping paperwork. The **customs broker** handles official border filings. In Australia the forwarder often includes this service. In China, the buyer usually hires the customs broker.

On the China side, the **importer** is the licensed company that brings goods into China. Your buyer is either the importer or works with one. The **distributor** buys goods in China and sells them onward to factories, wholesalers, or shops. One Chinese company can play both roles. Know exactly which roles your buyer plays before you sign anything.

<a id="ch03-3"></a>

### 3.3 Incoterms in plain English: EXW, FOB, CFR, CIF — who pays what, who risks what

**Incoterms** are standard three-letter trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce. Each term fixes two things: who pays each cost, and where risk passes from seller to buyer. **Risk** means responsibility for loss or damage.

Four terms cover most bean deals. **EXW** (Ex Works) means the buyer collects the goods at your door. The buyer pays and risks everything from there. **FOB** (Free On Board) means you pay all costs until the goods are loaded on the vessel at the Australian port. Risk passes to the buyer when the goods are on board.

**CFR** (Cost and Freight) means FOB plus you also pay the sea freight to the Chinese port. But risk still passes at the Australian port. **CIF** (Cost, Insurance and Freight) is CFR plus you also buy marine insurance for the voyage. Chinese buyers often ask for CIF prices because the price includes delivery to their port.

💰 **Example.** Sam sells 20 tonnes to a buyer in Guangzhou. His FOB Brisbane price is AU$25,300 for the lot. Sea freight is AU$2,000 and insurance is AU$200. So his CIF Nansha price is AU$25,300 + AU$2,000 + AU$200 = AU$27,500. Same beans, different term, different price. Always name the Incoterm and the port next to any price.

<a id="ch03-4"></a>

### 3.4 The money flow: deposits, telegraphic transfers, letters of credit

Money in export moves in a few standard ways. The workhorse is the **telegraphic transfer** (T/T). This is a normal international bank transfer. A **deposit** is a part payment made up front, before shipment. A common beginner pattern is a deposit before loading and the balance after shipping documents are ready.

The heavyweight tool is the **letter of credit** (L/C). This is a bank's promise to pay you when you present exact documents. The buyer's bank issues it. Your bank checks your documents against it. An L/C protects both sides but costs fees and demands perfect paperwork. [Chapter 13](#ch13) covers it in depth.

💰 **Example.** Sam's CIF price is AU$27,500. He agrees on 30% deposit by T/T, which is AU$8,250 before loading. The balance of AU$19,250 is due when he sends copies of the shipping documents. Sam does not release the original documents until the balance arrives. Without the originals, the buyer cannot collect the beans in China.

That last sentence is the core of export payment safety. Documents control the goods. Control the documents, and you control the deal.

<a id="ch03-5"></a>

### 3.5 The paper flow: every document in one shipment and why each exists

One shipment needs a small stack of documents. Each exists for a reason. The **commercial invoice** states what you sold, to whom, and for how much. Customs on both sides read it. The **packing list** states what is in the container: bag count, weights, and marks. Inspectors use it to check the cargo.

The **bill of lading** (B/L) is the shipping line's receipt for the cargo and the ticket to collect it. Whoever holds the original bill of lading controls the goods. This is why documents control payment. The **phytosanitary certificate** is a plant-health certificate from the Australian government. It tells China the beans were inspected and meet plant-health rules ([Section 5.3](#ch05-3)).

The **fumigation certificate** proves the container was treated against insects. The **Certificate of Origin** proves the goods are Australian, which earns tariff savings under the China–Australia trade agreement ([Section 12.4](#ch12-4)). A **tariff** is an import tax.

Some deals add more: an insurance certificate, a quality certificate from a lab, and the export declaration for the Australian border ([Section 5.5](#ch05-5)). Keep every document consistent. The same weights, names, and numbers must appear on all of them. One mismatch can hold a container for weeks.

## Chapter 4 — Realistic Expectations (Read Before Spending a Dollar)

<a id="ch04-1"></a>

### 4.1 💰 How much money you actually need: three starting budgets

💰 **Example.** Here are three toy budgets, using Sam's numbers. Budget one is the **sample budget: about AU$1,000**. This pays for small bean samples, courier fees to China, and a few lab tests. It buys learning and conversations, not trade.

Budget two is the **broker budget: about AU$5,000**. At this level Sam does not buy beans. He connects a buyer to an established exporter and earns a commission ([Section 9.2](#ch09-2)). The money covers company setup, phone calls, samples, travel to meet packers, and time.

Budget three is the **first-container budget: about AU$40,000**. Sam's container costs AU$25,000 all-in: AU$20,000 for 20 tonnes of beans at AU$1,000 per tonne, plus AU$5,000 in packing, freight, and fees ([Appendix C](#appc) shows every line). The extra AU$15,000 is a buffer. It covers late payment, extra tests, storage, and surprises. Never start a container with only the exact cost of the container.

⚠️ These are toy numbers for learning. Real bean prices, freight rates, and fees change constantly. Get live quotes before you budget.

<a id="ch04-2"></a>

### 4.2 Typical margins per container — and the costs that quietly eat them

Export margins are thin. A **margin** is your profit as a share of the deal. 💰 **Example.** Sam's total cost is AU$25,000. He sells CIF for AU$27,500. His profit is AU$2,500, a 10% margin on cost. That is a healthy toy outcome. Many real deals run thinner.

Now watch the quiet costs eat it. The forwarder adds a documentation fee. The port adds a terminal charge. The bank takes a transfer fee at each end, perhaps AU$50 here and AU$50 there. Currency moves 2% against you between contract and payment, which on AU$27,500 is AU$550 ([Section 13.3](#ch13-3)).

Then come the avoidable ones. **Demurrage** is a daily fee when your container sits too long at a port or terminal. A document error can trigger a week of it. A repeated lab test costs a few hundred dollars. A rushed truck booking costs extra.

The lesson: a 10% paper margin can become 4% in real life. Protect margin by getting every quote in writing, checking every document twice, and building the buffer from [Section 4.1](#ch04-1). Profit in this trade is not won in the selling price. It is defended in the details.

<a id="ch04-3"></a>

### 4.3 Timeframes: why this takes months, not weeks

New traders expect weeks. The trade takes months. Here is a realistic first-deal clock. Learning the basics and setting up your business takes about a month. Finding and testing a supplier takes another month, with samples and lab results moving back and forth.

Finding and vetting a real buyer takes one to three months. Trust between strangers grows slowly, especially across languages. Negotiating specs, price, and payment takes weeks more. Then execution begins: booking freight, packing, inspection, and documents take several weeks ([Section 12.1](#ch12-1) gives a 12-week countdown).

The voyage itself takes roughly two to three weeks to southern China. Customs clearance takes days when smooth, weeks when not. Final payment may land weeks after that. Add it up: from first study to money in the bank, six to nine months is normal for a first container.

Season matters too. Australian harvest runs roughly December to May. Buy too far from harvest and stock is scarce or old. Plan your calendar around the crop, not around your impatience. Rushing is the most expensive habit in this trade.

<a id="ch04-4"></a>

### 4.4 The honest failure modes: why most new traders quit in year one

Most new export traders quit within a year or so. Knowing why helps you avoid it. Failure mode one: undercapitalisation. That means starting without enough money. One delayed payment or one extra fee ends the business. The buffer in [Section 4.1](#ch04-1) exists for this.

Failure mode two: one buyer, one deal. The trader builds everything on a single relationship. The buyer goes quiet, and the business is zero. [Section 16.4](#ch16-4) treats diversification as survival, because it is.

Failure mode three: quality shock. The trader skips testing to save a few hundred dollars, and a container fails in China. [Chapter 14](#ch14) shows the cost. Failure mode four: fraud. The trader wires money to a fake buyer or fake supplier ([Chapter 15](#ch15)).

Failure mode five is the quiet one: exhaustion. Months of unpaid work, time zones, and slow replies wear people down. The defence is to start small, keep your day job at first, and treat year one as paid education. Survive year one, and your odds improve sharply.

<a id="ch04-5"></a>

### 4.5 Your unfair advantages: Australian residency, family in Iran, friends in China — and the limits of each

Sam's profile, like yours, carries real advantages. Australian residency is the first. You can open Australian bank accounts, register an Australian business, and deal with DAFF directly. Buyers trust Australian sellers with Australian paperwork. This advantage is legal and structural.

Friends in China are the second. Real people can introduce buyers, check a company's reputation, visit a warehouse, and translate culture, not just words. But friendship has limits. A friend is not a licensed importer, and a friendly promise is not a contract. [Section 11.1](#ch11-1) shows how to use this advantage without burning it.

Family in Iran is the third. Iran knows ماش well, and Iranian traders know pulses. Family can offer trade knowledge, contacts, and insight into Persian diaspora demand in other countries. But direct Iran trade faces hard limits: sanctions and banking barriers. **Sanctions** are legal bans on certain trade and payments. [Section 19.2](#ch19-2) explains why legal advice must come first there.

Use each advantage for what it truly is. Residency gives you the platform. Friends give you the introductions. Family gives you the knowledge. None of them replaces contracts, testing, and paperwork. Advantages open doors. Systems keep you alive inside them.

# PART II — THE RULEBOOK: Registrations, Licences, Paperwork

<a id="ch05"></a>

## Chapter 5 — The Australian Side (DAFF and Friends)

<a id="ch05-1"></a>

### 5.1 The Export Control Act 2020: where mung beans sit in the rules

Australia controls food exports through law. The main law is the **Export Control Act 2020**, with detailed rules underneath it. The government department in charge is **DAFF**, the Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry. DAFF's job is to make sure Australian exports meet the importing country's requirements, so Australia keeps its trade reputation.

Mung beans sit in the category called **plants and plant products**. This category covers grains, pulses, and seeds. A **pulse** is the dry edible seed of a legume, such as mung beans, chickpeas, and lentils. The rules for this category say when goods must be inspected, certified, and prepared in registered facilities.

For you, the practical meaning is this. You cannot simply fill a container with beans and ship it. The beans must be prepared correctly, the facility may need registration, and the shipment may need government inspection and certificates, depending on what China requires. DAFF publishes country requirements in its Manual of Importing Country Requirements, called **MICOR**. Check MICOR for China and mung beans before every planning step.

⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: DAFF at agriculture.gov.au, including MICOR.

<a id="ch05-2"></a>

### 5.2 Registered establishments: what must be registered — and what you can simply rent

A **registered establishment** is a facility that DAFF has approved for preparing export goods. Registration covers places that clean, grade, treat, or store goods for export where the rules require it. The facility must meet standards for hygiene, pest control, and record-keeping, and it gets audited.

Here is the good news for a small trader. You do not need to own a registered establishment. Packers across Queensland and New South Wales already hold registration. When you buy beans packed by a registered packer, you rent their compliance along with their machines. Their registration number appears in the export paperwork.

This is a core beginner strategy, and it repeats on the Chinese side in [Section 6.3](#ch06-3). Established facilities carry the licences. You carry the deal. You pay for that service in the packing fee, and it is worth every dollar.

Ask three questions before you choose a packer. Is your establishment registered with DAFF for pulse exports? Is it also registered on the Chinese side where needed? Can you show me recent audit results? A good packer answers fast. A vague answer is your cue to leave. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: agriculture.gov.au.

<a id="ch05-3"></a>

### 5.3 Phytosanitary certificates and export inspection

**Phytosanitary** means "about plant health". A **phytosanitary certificate** is an official government document. It certifies that an inspector checked the goods and that they meet the importing country's plant-health rules. China requires one for mung beans. Without it, your container will not clear Chinese customs.

The certificate is issued by DAFF, not by you and not by a private lab. Before issue, the goods pass an **export inspection**. An authorised officer examines samples from the lot. The officer looks for live insects, seeds of weed species, soil, and other contamination. Freedom from live insects is critical, because a single live insect can fail the inspection.

Inspection usually happens at the packer's registered establishment, at or after packing. This is another reason to use an experienced packer. They know the inspectors, the standards, and the paperwork rhythm. They fix problems before inspection day, not after.

Plan the timing. Inspection and certification must line up with your vessel booking. A failed inspection means treatment or re-cleaning, then re-inspection, and a missed ship. Build slack into your schedule ([Section 12.1](#ch12-1)). ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: agriculture.gov.au and MICOR for current China conditions.

<a id="ch05-4"></a>

### 5.4 NEXDOC/EXDOC: the electronic certification system, step by step

Export certificates are requested through a government computer system. The old system was called **EXDOC**. Its replacement is **NEXDOC**. Plant export certification has moved to NEXDOC. You may still hear old-timers say "EXDOC"; treat the names as one idea, the electronic certificate system.

Here is the flow in simple steps. Step one: someone lodges a Request for Permit, the electronic application that describes the exporter, the goods, the buyer, and the destination. Step two: the system checks the details against the rules for China and mung beans. Step three: inspection and any treatments are completed and recorded. Step four: the system issues the export permit and the phytosanitary certificate.

Now the practical secret: beginners almost never operate NEXDOC themselves. Your freight forwarder or packer usually holds system access and lodges everything for a fee. You supply accurate deal details: names, addresses, weights, container numbers, vessel details. They drive the system.

Your job is accuracy and timing. Give complete details early. One wrong digit in a container number can delay a certificate, and a late certificate can miss a vessel. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: agriculture.gov.au (search "NEXDOC").

<a id="ch05-5"></a>

### 5.5 Border basics: export declarations (EDN) and Australian Border Force

One more Australian agency stands at the wharf: the **Australian Border Force** (ABF). ABF controls what physically leaves the country. Almost every commercial export needs an **export declaration**, an electronic filing that describes the goods, their value, and their destination. The system returns an **EDN**, an Export Declaration Number.

The EDN is a small thing that blocks big things. The shipping line needs it before your container can board the vessel. No EDN, no loading. Like NEXDOC, this filing is routine work for your freight forwarder or customs broker. You will rarely touch the system yourself.

The declaration also feeds trade statistics and enforces export laws, including sanctions law. Declare honestly and exactly. Wrong values or wrong descriptions are offences, even when accidental sloppiness caused them.

Keep copies of every declaration with your shipment file. [Section 7.5](#ch07-5) covers record-keeping. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: abf.gov.au.

## Chapter 6 — The Chinese Side (GACC and CIFER)

<a id="ch06-1"></a>

### 6.1 Decrees 248 → 280: what changed and what actually matters to you

China's customs authority is **GACC**, the General Administration of Customs of China. In 2021, GACC issued Decree 248, a rule that changed food imports worldwide. A **decree** is a numbered official regulation. Decree 248 requires overseas food facilities to be registered with GACC before their products can enter China. A companion decree covered import food safety in general.

GACC has continued to update this system with later decrees, and you may see references to newer decree numbers, including 280, replacing or amending earlier ones. Do not memorise decree numbers. Memorise the principle: China wants every foreign food facility in its registration database, and the details of who registers and how can change.

What actually matters to you is narrow. First: is the facility that processes and packs your mung beans registered with GACC? Second: does the registration cover the right product category? Third: do your shipping documents and packaging show the registration number where required?

Those three questions decide whether your container clears. Everything else in the decrees is background noise for a small trader. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: GACC at customs.gov.cn and DAFF's guidance at agriculture.gov.au.

<a id="ch06-2"></a>

### 6.2 CIFER registration: who must register (spoiler: often not you, if you're a pure trader)

**CIFER** is the China Import Food Enterprise Registration system. It is the online database where overseas food facilities register under GACC's decrees. Each registered facility gets a Chinese registration number. That number follows the goods on documents and sometimes on packaging.

Now the spoiler from the heading. Registration attaches to facilities that produce, process, and store food, not to every company in the chain. A **pure trader** buys and sells goods but never physically handles them. If you are a pure trader, the packer's facility usually carries the registration, not your office.

For many plant products, including pulses, registration runs through the home government. DAFF recommends or submits Australian facilities to GACC rather than each facility applying alone. This is another reason the established packer is your friend. They and DAFF have already done the hard part.

Your job is verification, not registration. Confirm the packer's CIFER status and number before you contract. Ask for the number, then check it in the CIFER public query or ask DAFF. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: cifer.singlewindow.cn and agriculture.gov.au.

<a id="ch06-3"></a>

### 6.3 The trader shortcut: sourcing from already-CIFER-registered facilities

Put [Section 5.2](#ch05-2) and [Section 6.2](#ch06-2) together and you get the single most important structural trick in this book. Buy beans packed at a facility that is registered on both sides: DAFF-registered in Australia and CIFER-registered in China. Then you inherit compliance on both ends of the voyage.

Call this the **trader shortcut**. It converts two bureaucratic mountains into one supplier-selection question. The Australian Mung Bean Association lists accredited processors, which is a strong starting list ([Section 10.3](#ch10-3)).

Follow a simple verification routine. Ask the packer for their DAFF establishment number and their CIFER registration number in writing. Confirm the CIFER entry covers the right product category for mung beans. Ask which Chinese ports they have shipped to recently. Ask your buyer to confirm the numbers clear smoothly at their port.

The shortcut has one price: dependence. If your packer loses registration or capacity, your exports stop. So keep relationships with at least two qualified packers from early on. Diversification logic starts here, not just with buyers ([Section 16.4](#ch16-4)).

<a id="ch06-4"></a>

### 6.4 China's MRLs, labelling and packaging rules for pulses

China sets food safety limits in national standards called **GB standards**. GB 2763 is the standard for pesticide MRLs. As [Section 1.4](#ch01-4) explained, China's limits can be stricter than Australia's, and glyphosate on pulses is the famous trap. Your pre-shipment lab test must check results against the Chinese limits, not the Australian ones.

Packaging has rules too. Export bags must be new, clean, and strong enough for the voyage. Woven polypropylene bags of 25 kilograms are the workhorse. Marks on the bags typically show the product name, origin, weight, and the facility registration number where required.

Labelling requirements depend on how the goods enter China. Bulk raw materials for factory processing face simpler rules than retail packs. If you ever sell consumer-ready packets, Chinese-language retail labelling rules apply in full, with strict format requirements. Start with bulk. Leave retail packs until you have a partner who owns that compliance.

Ask two parties to confirm current requirements before each shipment: your packer, who bags and marks, and your buyer, who clears customs. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: customs.gov.cn and the GB standards via China's standards portals; DAFF's MICOR summarises China's conditions.

<a id="ch06-5"></a>

### 6.5 Your buyer's side: import licences, quarantine, and customs inspection

Your buyer has homework too, and their homework can sink your shipment. First, licences. A Chinese company needs import rights and food-business registrations to import food. Some products also need an inspection and quarantine permit obtained before shipment. Confirm early which permits your buyer needs for mung beans at their port, and see the permit before you ship.

Second, quarantine. **Quarantine** is the plant-health control system at the border. Chinese officers can inspect the container, sample the beans, and send samples to a lab. They check insects, weed seeds, disease, and residues. Your phytosanitary certificate ([Section 5.3](#ch05-3)) is the passport into this process, not an exemption from it.

Third, customs inspection in the wider sense: documents, values, and tariff classification. The buyer's customs broker lodges the import declaration and pays duties and taxes. Errors or mismatches trigger holds. Holds cost storage and demurrage, and someone must pay ([Section 14.3](#ch14-3)).

A capable buyer treats all this as routine. A weak buyer improvises. When you vet buyers in [Chapter 11](#ch11), you are mostly vetting their ability to do this exact homework. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: customs.gov.cn.

## Chapter 7 — Setting Up Your Business Properly

<a id="ch07-1"></a>

### 7.1 Sole trader vs company: structure, ABN, and GST on exports

Australian law gives you two practical structures. A **sole trader** is one person doing business in their own name. It is cheap and simple, but the person carries every debt personally. A **company** (a Pty Ltd) is a separate legal person. It costs more to set up and run, but it limits your personal risk if a deal goes badly wrong.

Export carries real liability: a rejected container, an unpaid supplier, a contract dispute. For that reason, most serious traders form a company early. Talk to an accountant before you choose. This book is not legal or tax advice.

Whatever the structure, you need an **ABN**, an Australian Business Number. It is a free registration that identifies your business to government, banks, packers, and forwarders. You will also meet **GST**, the 10% Goods and Services Tax. Here is the good export news: exports are generally GST-free. You do not add GST to your export invoice.

Even better, you can usually claim back GST you paid on Australian costs, such as packing and transport, through your business activity statements. Register for GST, keep every tax invoice, and let your accountant reclaim what is yours. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: ato.gov.au and abr.gov.au.

<a id="ch07-2"></a>

### 7.2 Insurance: marine cargo, product liability, trade credit

Three insurance types matter in this trade. First, **marine cargo insurance**. It covers physical loss or damage to your goods in transit: water damage, a dropped container, a fire, even a ship lost at sea. If you sell CIF, the contract requires you to buy it. It is priced as a small percentage of cargo value. In Sam's toy numbers it is AU$200 on a AU$27,500 container.

Second, **product liability insurance**. It covers claims that your product harmed someone. Food carries this risk by nature. Many buyers and platforms ask for proof of it.

Third, **trade credit insurance**. It covers you when a buyer fails to pay. Insurers assess your buyer and set a credit limit. Small new exporters may find cover limited or costly, but a quote is free, and the insurer's risk assessment of your buyer is useful information by itself.

⚠️ Know what insurance does not cover. Standard marine cargo insurance does not pay because Chinese customs rejected your beans for residue. That risk is managed by testing and contracts ([Section 14.3](#ch14-3)). Read exclusions before you rely on any policy.

<a id="ch07-3"></a>

### 7.3 Banking: foreign currency accounts and receiving money from China

Your bank setup decides how smoothly money arrives. Start with a business transaction account in Australian dollars under your company name. Buyers pay a company account more willingly than a personal account, and regulators expect clean separation of business money.

Next, consider a **foreign currency account**, an account that holds another currency, usually US dollars. Much of world commodity trade is priced in US dollars. If a buyer pays USD into your AUD account, the bank converts it instantly at the bank's rate. A USD account lets you hold the dollars and choose your conversion moment ([Section 13.3](#ch13-3)).

Compare providers on transfer fees and exchange-rate margins, because banks differ widely. Specialist foreign-exchange providers often beat the big banks on rate. Check that any provider is licensed in Australia.

Expect compliance questions. Banks screen international payments under anti-money-laundering law. Payments from China may trigger questions about the sender and the purpose. Clean contracts and invoices answer those questions fast. Also note China's own rules: your buyer faces currency controls when sending money out of China, which is one more reason to vet buyer capability ([Section 11.2](#ch11-2)).

<a id="ch07-4"></a>

### 7.4 Free government help: Austrade, EMDG grants, state trade offices

Australia funds real help for new exporters. Use it. **Austrade** is the federal trade agency. It publishes export guides, runs events, and has people who know the China food market. Its website is a strong free education (austrade.gov.au).

The **EMDG** program, Export Market Development Grants, gives money back to eligible small exporters for marketing costs: trade fair travel, promotion, and similar spending. Grant rules and funding rounds change often, so read the current guidelines before you plan around it. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: austrade.gov.au (search "EMDG").

State governments run trade offices too. Queensland's trade arm matters most for mung beans, since the crop grows there. State offices run missions to China, host buyer delegations, and sometimes fund market visits. Their staff know which packers and exporters are active.

Add two industry bodies to your free-help list. Grain Trade Australia sets the standard contracts you will use ([Section 8.1](#ch08-1)). The Australian Mung Bean Association connects the actual industry ([Section 10.3](#ch10-3)). Membership fees are small against what you learn.

<a id="ch07-5"></a>

### 7.5 Record-keeping that keeps you audit-proof

Boring record-keeping is a survival skill in export. Several parties can audit you: the tax office, DAFF, your bank, an insurer, or a court in a dispute. **Audit-proof** means your records answer any question fast, with documents, not memory.

Build one folder per shipment from day one. Into it goes everything: the contract, all emails and chat messages about specs, lab reports, the invoice, the packing list, the bill of lading, certificates, bank records, and the export declaration. When chat apps like WeChat carry deal terms, export or screenshot the key messages into the folder. Chat history is evidence, but only if you keep it.

Keep records for at least five years, which aligns with Australian tax rules. Store them in the cloud with backup, not on one laptop. Name files consistently: date, shipment number, document type.

The payoff is speed under pressure. When a container is held in China, the trader who can produce the full document pack in ten minutes wins arguments and saves demurrage days. When the bank queries a payment, one PDF answers it. Records are not paperwork. Records are ammunition.

## Chapter 8 — Contracts That Protect You

<a id="ch08-1"></a>

### 8.1 Grain Trade Australia (GTA) standard contracts: your ready-made armour

**Grain Trade Australia** (GTA) is the industry body for Australian grain trading. Its most valuable products are its standard contracts and trade rules. A **standard contract** is a pre-written set of terms that the whole industry knows and courts and arbitrators understand. Decades of disputes have already tested its wording.

Why does this matter to a beginner? Because a beginner cannot out-draft a lawyer, and a one-page email deal protects nobody. When you buy beans from a packer or trader, expect the deal to reference GTA terms. Use the same armour when you sell. Your sale contract to China can state that GTA rules and Australian law govern the deal, with arbitration as the dispute path ([Section 8.4](#ch08-4)).

A standard contract still needs your inputs: the parties, the goods, the specs, the quantity, the price, the Incoterm, the shipment window, and the payment terms. The standard terms handle the machinery around those inputs: quality procedures, notices, default, and disputes.

Read one full GTA contract slowly, once, before your first deal. It is the cheapest trade education available. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: graintrade.org.au.

<a id="ch08-2"></a>

### 8.2 Quality specs in writing: sampling, testing, tolerances

A quality dispute without a written spec is a shouting match. A quality dispute with a written spec is a procedure. Your contract must define three things: the specification, the sampling method, and the testing authority.

The **specification** lists measurable numbers from [Section 1.3](#ch01-3): moisture limit, defect limits, foreign material limit, size, and, when relevant, germination rate and residue limits against Chinese MRLs. Write numbers, not adjectives. "Good quality beans" is not a spec. "Moisture max 14%" is a spec.

**Sampling** states how and when the test sample is taken, and by whom. The honest standard is sampling at loading by an independent inspection company. **Testing** states which lab's result is final. Name a specific independent lab in the contract.

Then add **tolerances** and remedies. A tolerance is a small allowed deviation with an agreed price adjustment instead of rejection. Example: moisture up to 0.5% over the limit triggers a price allowance, not cancellation. Tolerances turn small problems into arithmetic instead of warfare. Rejection rights then apply only to serious failures, which protects both sides.

<a id="ch08-3"></a>

### 8.3 Payment terms a beginner should accept — and ones to refuse

Payment terms decide who is exposed and for how long. As a beginner seller, accept terms where you never lose control of the goods before money is secured. The beginner-safe pattern from [Section 3.4](#ch03-4): a deposit before loading, the balance paid before you release the original bill of lading. A confirmed letter of credit is also acceptable once you can manage documents precisely ([Section 13.2](#ch13-2)).

💰 **Example.** Sam's contract says: 30% deposit (AU$8,250) within five days of signing; balance (AU$19,250) against copies of shipping documents; originals released only after full payment. His worst case is a cancelled deal with beans still under his control, not beans gone and money missing.

Now the refusal list. Refuse **open account** terms, meaning payment weeks after the buyer receives the goods. That is a loan from you to a foreign stranger. Refuse "balance after our quality inspection at our warehouse", which lets a buyer invent discounts after the goods arrive. Refuse payments to third-party accounts that do not match the buyer's company name, a classic fraud pattern ([Chapter 15](#ch15)).

Buyers who insist on refusal-list terms are telling you your risk does not matter to them. Believe them, and walk away politely.

<a id="ch08-4"></a>

### 8.4 When deals go wrong: arbitration and what "recourse" really means across borders

**Recourse** means your realistic ability to get money back when the other side breaks the deal. Across borders, recourse is weaker than beginners assume. Suing a Chinese company in an Australian court is usually pointless: even a win is hard to enforce in China. Court is slow, public, and expensive.

Trade uses **arbitration** instead. Arbitration is private dispute resolution where an appointed expert panel hears both sides and issues a binding decision, called an award. Both Australia and China belong to the international treaty (the New York Convention) that makes foreign arbitration awards broadly enforceable. Your contract must name arbitration, the rules, and the place before any dispute exists. GTA arbitration is the natural choice for Australian grain deals; some China deals name CIETAC, a major Chinese arbitration body.

Now the honesty. For one 20-tonne container, formal arbitration can cost a large share of the claim. Real-world recourse at your size is layered: prevention first, then negotiation, then commercial pressure such as industry reputation, then formal arbitration as the last layer.

This is why the whole chapter points backward. The deposit is recourse. The document control is recourse. The named lab is recourse. A contract's best outcome is the dispute that never starts because the terms left no room for games.

## Chapter 9 — The Micro-Start Strategy

<a id="ch09-1"></a>

### 9.1 Level 0: samples by courier to your friends in China

Level 0 is trade with training wheels. You send small bean samples to China by courier and learn from what happens. A **sample** is a small quantity, typically one to two kilograms, that shows the product's quality. Buy graded samples from packers, with the spec sheet and, ideally, a recent lab report attached.

The goals are learning goals. You learn how packers respond to a small new customer. You learn what your Chinese contacts say about size, colour, and price. You learn courier paperwork, because even samples cross borders with declarations, and food samples can face import rules on the Chinese side. Ask the courier company about current requirements for food samples to China before you send.

💰 **Example.** Sam orders three graded samples from two packers. Each sample plus courier costs about AU$100. For about AU$500 total, Sam gets real product in real Chinese hands, plus first impressions from two sprout wholesalers his friend knows in Guangzhou.

Level 0 cannot make money. It can only make knowledge and relationships. That is the point. Spend your first AU$1,000 here before you risk AU$40,000 anywhere.

<a id="ch09-2"></a>

### 9.2 Level 1: piggyback — broker a deal through an established exporter for a commission

Level 1 removes the scariest parts of the trade from your plate. You find a genuine Chinese buyer, then bring that buyer to an established Australian exporter. The exporter runs the deal: sourcing, packing, certification, shipping, and payment risk. You earn a commission for creating the connection. This is the **piggyback**: your deal rides on their licences, systems, and balance sheet.

Why would an exporter share margin with you? Because real buyers are the scarce resource. A trusted new buyer relationship is worth a commission to any exporter with spare capacity.

Protect yourself with one document: a **commission agreement** signed before you introduce the buyer. It names the buyer you are introducing, the commission rate, the payment trigger, and the duration, including repeat orders. Without it, you can be thanked and bypassed.

💰 **Example.** Sam introduces a Guangzhou buyer to an established exporter. They sign a commission agreement at AU$25 per tonne. The buyer takes one 20-tonne container. Sam earns 20 × AU$25 = AU$500. Small money, but Sam watched a full container cycle from inside, at zero cargo risk. That education normally costs much more.

<a id="ch09-3"></a>

### 9.3 Level 2: your own first container — the 20-foot milestone

Level 2 is the real thing: you buy the beans, you sell the beans, your name is on the export. The 20-foot container is the natural first milestone. It is the smallest standard sea-freight unit for this trade, about 20 to 25 tonnes of bagged beans. In this book's toy numbers, Sam's version is 20 tonnes and AU$25,000 of total cost.

Level 2 changes your exposure completely. At Levels 0 and 1, your worst case was wasted time and a few hundred dollars. At Level 2, your capital is inside a steel box on the ocean, subject to quality risk, payment risk, and rejection risk. Every chapter of Parts II and IV now applies to you personally.

Move to Level 2 only when three things are true. You have a vetted buyer with a signed contract and a deposit ([Chapter 11](#ch11)). You have a dual-registered packer and a tested lot ([Chapter 10](#ch10)). You have the full budget including buffer ([Section 4.1](#ch04-1)).

[Chapter 12](#ch12) is the complete playbook for this milestone. Read it twice before you commit money once.

<a id="ch09-4"></a>

### 9.4 ✅ Decision tree: which level are you actually ready for?

✅ Answer these questions honestly. They form a decision tree.

- [ ] **Question 1.** Have I read Parts I and II and can I explain MRLs, Incoterms, and the document pack without notes? If no → stay at study. If yes → go to Question 2.
- [ ] **Question 2.** Have real samples of graded Australian beans reached real Chinese contacts, with feedback on spec and price? If no → **Level 0** is your level. If yes → go to Question 3.
- [ ] **Question 3.** Do I have a genuine buyer, and do I have either the capital buffer or the experience of a full container cycle? If buyer yes but capital or experience no → **Level 1**: piggyback and get paid to learn. If buyer yes and capital yes and I have seen a full cycle → go to Question 4.
- [ ] **Question 4.** Do I have a signed contract, a deposit, a dual-registered packer, and a tested lot? If yes to all → **Level 2**: execute Chapter 12. If any no → fix that gap first; do not ship around it.

The tree has no shame at any node. Every level is a legitimate business stage. Skipping a level is how beginners donate money to the ocean.

✅ **Chapter 9 checklist**

- [ ] I know the three levels: samples, piggyback commission, own container.
- [ ] I will spend my first AU$1,000 on Level 0 learning.
- [ ] I will sign a commission agreement before any Level 1 introduction.
- [ ] I will enter Level 2 only through the decision tree, with no skipped answers.

## Chapter 10 — Sourcing Beans in Australia

<a id="ch10-1"></a>

### 10.1 Where the beans grow: the Queensland and northern NSW belt

Australian mung beans grow in a belt of country that runs through Queensland and into northern New South Wales. Key areas include the Darling Downs around Toowoomba and Dalby, central Queensland around Emerald, and the Burdekin and other northern districts. Rain and season shift the mix from year to year.

Geography is kind to a Brisbane beginner like Sam. The growing belt, the major packers, and the Port of Brisbane sit within a day's drive of each other. You can meet a packer, inspect a facility, and see the port in one trip. Do that trip. Faces and handshakes still move this industry.

The belt matters for risk too. Mung beans are a summer crop that depends on summer rain. A wet harvest can stain beans and cut quality. A drought can cut the whole crop. Prices and availability swing with the weather in this one region, so watch its seasons like a farmer even though you are a trader.

Plan your buying calendar around harvest, roughly December to May. Fresh-harvest months give the best choice of quality and the most competition among sellers. Late in the season, quality narrows and holders of good beans know their power.

<a id="ch10-2"></a>

### 10.2 Growers vs packers vs traders: who to buy from at your size

You can buy from three kinds of seller. Buying direct from a **grower** looks cheapest, and the romance is real: paddock to Guangzhou. But raw beans off the header are uncleaned, ungraded, and untested. You would still need a packer for cleaning, grading, bagging, and export compliance. Direct grower deals suit larger traders who can manage that chain.

Buying from a **packer** costs more per tonne, but the price buys the whole compliance stack from Part II: cleaning, grading to spec, bagging, DAFF registration, inspection experience, and often CIFER registration. For a first container, the packer is the right seller almost every time. This is the trader shortcut in action ([Section 6.3](#ch06-3)).

Buying from another **trader** means buying beans that already carry someone's margin. Sometimes that is fine: a trader may hold exactly the grade you need, ready to ship, when packers are sold out. Vet a trader seller as carefully as you would vet a buyer.

At Sam's size, the ranking is clear. Packer first. Trader when the packer cannot supply. Grower-direct later, when volume justifies managing the chain yourself ([Chapter 18](#ch18)).

<a id="ch10-3"></a>

### 10.3 The Australian Mung Bean Association and accredited processors

The **Australian Mung Bean Association** (AMA) is the industry body for Australian mung beans. It links growers, agronomists, packers, and exporters. It maintains quality standards for Australian mung bean grades, runs an accreditation program, and promotes the crop overseas.

The word to remember is **accredited**. An accredited processor has been assessed against the AMA's standards for handling and grading mung beans. The AMA's list of accredited members is effectively a pre-vetted supplier directory for a beginner. It does not replace your own checks, but it filters the field to serious operators.

Use the AMA three ways. First, source from its accredited processor list, cross-checked for DAFF and CIFER registration ([Section 6.3](#ch06-3)). Second, learn from its published standards, which define the grades your contracts will reference ([Section 1.1](#ch01-1)). Third, join and show up. Industry events put you in rooms with people whose knowledge is not on any website.

A small industry notices newcomers fast. Arrive prepared, honest about your size, and reliable in small commitments. Reputation compounds in both directions here. ⚠️ Details change. Check the official source: mungbean.org.au.

<a id="ch10-4"></a>

### 10.4 ✅ The buying checklist: specs, residue certificates, germination tests

✅ Run this checklist on every lot you consider buying.

- [ ] **Spec sheet in writing.** Grade name, size, colour description, moisture, defect percentages, foreign material. Numbers, not adjectives ([Section 8.2](#ch08-2)).
- [ ] **Dual registration confirmed.** The packing facility's DAFF establishment number and CIFER registration number, in writing, current ([Section 6.3](#ch06-3)).
- [ ] **Residue certificate for this lot.** A recent lab report on the actual lot, checked against Chinese MRLs, glyphosate included ([Section 1.4](#ch01-4)).
- [ ] **Germination test if selling sprouting grade.** A current germination percentage on this lot, matching what your buyer's spec demands ([Section 1.3](#ch01-3)).
- [ ] **Sample retained and sealed.** You hold a sealed sample of the lot, labelled and dated, for comparison if a dispute comes later.

A seller who resists any line is answering a question you did not ask. Good packers produce these documents as routine. The checklist takes one email to send and can save an entire container.

<a id="ch10-5"></a>

### 10.5 Pre-shipment lab testing: the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy

Here is the habit that separates survivors from casualties: test the actual export lot, again, just before shipment. Not the paddock sample from harvest. Not the packer's stock test from three months ago. The blended, bagged lot that is about to enter your container.

Why twice? Because beans move and mix. Packers blend paddocks to hit a grade. Storage changes moisture. The 10-container war story in [Section 2.4](#ch02-4) died on exactly this gap: the tested beans and the shipped beans were not the same beans.

Use an accredited independent laboratory, and tell the lab the destination is China so results are compared against Chinese MRLs. In Australia, look for NATA accreditation, the national lab accreditation system. Test residues including glyphosate, moisture, and, for sprouting sales, germination. Ask your buyer if their customs experience suggests extra analytes to include.

💰 **Example.** Sam's pre-shipment panel costs about AU$500 against a AU$25,000 container: 2% of cost. If the test fails, Sam loses a booking fee and finds new beans. If an untested container fails in China instead, Sam loses months and most of AU$25,000. Cheapest insurance he will ever buy.

✅ **Chapter 10 checklist**

- [ ] I know the growing belt and buy to the harvest calendar.
- [ ] I buy from packers first at my size, growers later.
- [ ] I use the AMA accredited list, cross-checked for dual registration.
- [ ] I run the five-line buying checklist on every lot.
- [ ] I lab-test the actual export lot before every shipment.

## Chapter 11 — Finding and Vetting Your Chinese Buyer

<a id="ch11-1"></a>

### 11.1 Turning friends into business partners — the right way, and the friendship-destroying way

Your friends in China are a door, not a shortcut past the rules. The friendship-destroying way looks like this: vague verbal deals, favours instead of contracts, family-style trust instead of specifications, and silence about money until money goes wrong. When a quality dispute or late payment arrives, and one always arrives, neither side has a document to point to. The deal dies, and the friendship dies with it.

The right way separates roles cleanly. A friend can be an introducer, a translator, a local guide, or a paid agent. Each role deserves definition. If a friend introduces a buyer, agree openly whether an introduction fee applies. If a friend works ongoing, make it a written agent agreement with defined duties and commission ([Section 19.1](#ch19-1)).

Then apply one rule without exception: paper does not shrink because affection grows. A contract with a friend's company carries the same specs, the same payment terms, and the same named lab as a contract with a stranger. Explain it warmly: "Clear paper protects our friendship from business."

A true friend accepts that logic instantly. Someone who pushes back with "don't you trust me?" has told you something important. Trust is the reason to do the deal. It is never the reason to skip the contract.

<a id="ch11-2"></a>

### 11.2 Verifying an importer: licences, track record, references

Before you rely on any buyer, verify four layers. Layer one: legal existence. Every Chinese company has a **Unified Social Credit Code**, an 18-character registration number. Ask for the company's business licence, which shows this code, its legal name in Chinese, and its registered scope. Check it against China's public company registry, or pay a small fee for a company credit report from a commercial service.

Layer two: import capability. Confirm the buyer holds import rights and the food-related registrations from [Section 6.5](#ch06-5), and confirm which port they clear through. A genuine importer answers with documents, fast.

Layer three: track record. Ask what they import, from which countries, and how often. Ask for evidence: past bills of lading with sensitive parts hidden are a common, reasonable proof. Your Chinese friends can help here in ways no database can: a phone call to market contacts about the buyer's reputation.

Layer four: references and behaviour. Ask for one supplier reference from another country and actually call it. Then watch behaviour: real buyers ask detailed questions about spec, packing, and documents. Fake buyers rush past details toward your bank transfer ([Chapter 15](#ch15)).

<a id="ch11-3"></a>

### 11.3 Where buyers are found: trade fairs, B2B platforms, brokers, WeChat

Buyers exist in four hunting grounds. First, trade fairs. The China Import and Export Fair, called the **Canton Fair**, runs in Guangzhou each spring and autumn, and food-industry fairs run across Asia. A fair compresses a year of cold emails into three days of face-to-face meetings. Bring samples, bilingual spec sheets, and patience.

Second, B2B platforms. A **B2B platform** is an online marketplace for businesses, such as Alibaba's international site or Made-in-China. Platforms deliver volume and noise together: many enquiries, few genuine, some fraudulent. Treat platforms as lead generators only, then verify off-platform through [Section 11.2](#ch11-2).

Third, brokers. A **broker** matches buyers and sellers for a commission. Established grain brokers know real Chinese pulse buyers personally. Their commission is often the cheapest verified-buyer service you can buy.

Fourth, **WeChat**, China's dominant chat app. Business in China lives on it, and pulse-trade groups hum with offers and enquiries daily. Have your Chinese friends bring you into relevant groups; a personal introduction inside WeChat is worth fifty cold platform enquiries. Whatever the channel, the funnel ends the same way: verification, samples, small first deal.

<a id="ch11-4"></a>

### 11.4 Price negotiation: reading the CIF China price against your true costs

Negotiate from arithmetic, not hope. Build your **cost-up price**: every cost from bean to buyer's port, plus your target margin. Sam's cost-up from [Appendix C](#appc): AU$25,000 total cost for 20 tonnes, so AU$1,250 per tonne CIF break-even, and AU$1,375 per tonne at his 10% target.

Then read the market price backward. Buyers quote and compare in CIF China terms, often in US dollars per tonne. Ask several sources for current CIF China levels for Australian mung beans of your grade: your packer, a broker, and a friendly buyer. Convert to Australian dollars carefully ([Section 13.3](#ch13-3)).

💰 **Example.** The market tells Sam that Australian sprouting grade trades near AU$1,400 CIF China this month. His 10% target price is AU$1,375. He has room: he can hold near AU$1,400 and still beat his target. If the market said AU$1,250, his margin would be zero, and the correct move is not a cheaper contract. The correct move is no contract this month.

⚠️ Buyers test beginners with "market is much lower" claims. Answer with data, or walk. A trader who cannot walk away is not negotiating; he is donating.

<a id="ch11-5"></a>

### 11.5 Guanxi without naivety: relationships and contracts, together

**Guanxi** (关系) is the Chinese term for a network of relationships and mutual obligation. In China trade, guanxi is real infrastructure. Relationships decide whose calls are answered, whose containers get attention when a problem appears, and who hears about opportunities early. Dinner, tea, small gifts, and remembered favours are how this infrastructure is built and maintained.

The naive Western mistake is to dismiss guanxi as corruption or fluff. The naive newcomer mistake is the opposite: believing warm dinners replace cold documents. Chinese professionals sign detailed contracts with each other every day. The largest Chinese companies employ armies of lawyers. Relationship and contract are not rivals in Chinese business. They are two hands.

So run both tracks deliberately. Invest in the relationship: visit, share meals, introduce useful contacts, remember families and festivals, and honour every small promise, because small reliability is how guanxi measures you. And paper every deal: specs, terms, named labs, arbitration clauses, exactly as [Chapter 8](#ch08) prescribes.

The combination is the point. Guanxi without contracts leaves you defenceless on the bad day. Contracts without guanxi leave you last in line on every busy day. Together they make you a partner people both like and respect.

✅ **Chapter 11 checklist**

- [ ] I separate friendship roles from contract terms, in writing, warmly.
- [ ] I verify buyers in four layers: existence, capability, track record, references.
- [ ] I hunt in all four grounds: fairs, platforms, brokers, WeChat.
- [ ] I negotiate from cost-up arithmetic against real CIF quotes, and I can walk.
- [ ] I build guanxi and contracts together, never one without the other.

## Chapter 12 — Executing Container #1: The Step-by-Step Playbook

<a id="ch12-1"></a>

### 12.1 ✅ The 12-week countdown checklist

✅ Here is the countdown from decision to departure. Weeks are approximate; sequence is not.

- [ ] **Weeks 12–10.** Sign the sale contract with deposit terms ([Section 8.3](#ch08-3)). Receive the deposit. Confirm the packer can supply the grade in the window, with dual registration verified ([Section 10.4](#ch10-4)).
- [ ] **Weeks 10–8.** Contract the beans with the packer on GTA terms. Book freight with a forwarder ([Section 12.2](#ch12-2)). Confirm the buyer's import permits are in place ([Section 6.5](#ch06-5)).
- [ ] **Weeks 8–6.** Order the pre-shipment lab tests on the export lot ([Section 10.5](#ch10-5)). Confirm bag marks and packaging meet Chinese requirements ([Section 6.4](#ch06-4)).
- [ ] **Weeks 6–4.** Results in and clean. Lock the packing date. Arrange export inspection and certification through the packer or forwarder ([Section 5.3](#ch05-3), [Section 5.4](#ch05-4)).
- [ ] **Weeks 4–2.** Packing, fumigation, container loading, sealing ([Section 12.3](#ch12-3)). Draft all documents and send drafts to the buyer for checking.
- [ ] **Weeks 2–0.** Container to port. EDN filed ([Section 5.5](#ch05-5)). Vessel loads. Collect the full document pack ([Section 12.4](#ch12-4)). Invoice the balance.

Print this list. Tick it with a pen. Every tick is money defended.

<a id="ch12-2"></a>

### 12.2 Booking freight: forwarders, shipping lines, container choices

You will not call a shipping line yourself. Your **freight forwarder** does that, holding accounts with the lines and buying space at rates a beginner cannot get. Choose a forwarder who regularly ships agricultural containers from Brisbane to China. Ask exactly that question, plus whether they handle export documentation and EDN filing in-house.

The forwarder will ask you for the **shipping instruction**: product, weight, bag count, container type, destination port, consignee details, and required documents. Answer precisely; these details flow onto the bill of lading, and errors on a bill of lading are expensive to fix after sailing.

Container choice is mercifully simple at your size. A standard 20-foot general-purpose container fits about 20 to 25 tonnes of bagged beans, and weight limits, not space, set the ceiling. Ask the packer for their standard load plan. Confirm the container arrives clean, dry, and food-suitable; insist on rejection of any container with smells, rust flakes, or old cargo residue.

💰 **Example.** Sam's forwarder quotes AU$2,000 sea freight Brisbane to Nansha, plus local charges inside his AU$5,000 non-bean cost stack ([Appendix C](#appc)). The quote is valid for two weeks. Freight rates move like weather. Get quotes in writing, dated.

<a id="ch12-3"></a>

### 12.3 Fumigation, sealing, and loading day

**Fumigation** is pest treatment of the cargo or container with an approved gas, commonly phosphine for grain. China's requirements and your buyer's instructions determine what treatment is needed; your packer and forwarder will know the current standard. The treatment must be done by licensed operators who issue the **fumigation certificate** for your document pack.

Loading day is where paper meets steel. The container arrives at the packer. Someone checks it: clean, dry, no holes, no smell, weight-legal. Bags are counted as they load, stacked to the packer's plan so weight sits evenly. Photograph everything: the empty container, the part-loaded stack, the full load, the closed doors.

Then the **seal**: a numbered one-time metal lock applied to the container doors. The seal number is recorded on the bill of lading and other documents. An intact matching seal at destination proves nobody opened the box mid-journey. Photograph the applied seal so the number is readable.

If your contract or your caution demands it, an independent inspection company can attend loading, sample the cargo, and issue a loading report ([Section 8.2](#ch08-2)). For a first container, this small fee buys a neutral witness. Disputes that reach a neutral witness usually end quickly.

<a id="ch12-4"></a>

### 12.4 The document pack: invoice, packing list, bill of lading, phyto, Certificate of Origin (ChAFTA tariff savings), fumigation certificate

Assemble the pack you met in [Section 3.5](#ch03-5), now for real. The commercial invoice and packing list you or your forwarder produce. The bill of lading comes from the shipping line after sailing. The phytosanitary certificate comes from DAFF via NEXDOC ([Section 5.4](#ch05-4)). The fumigation certificate comes from the fumigator ([Section 12.3](#ch12-3)).

Now the money document beginners forget. **ChAFTA** is the China–Australia Free Trade Agreement, in force since 2015. Under ChAFTA, Australian-origin goods enter China at reduced or zero tariff rates, and mung beans benefit. The key is the **Certificate of Origin** (COO): the document proving Australian origin, issued by an authorised body such as an Australian chamber of commerce, in the ChAFTA format.

Without the COO, your buyer pays the full tariff rate, and that cost lands back on your price competitiveness. With it, the buyer saves real percentage points. Order the COO as routine, cost a small fee per shipment, and send a draft to the buyer before final issue.

Before releasing anything, run the consistency check: identical weights, names, container number, and seal number across every document. Then follow your payment terms exactly: copies to trigger the balance, originals only after money ([Section 8.3](#ch08-3)). ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: dfat.gov.au for ChAFTA details.

<a id="ch12-5"></a>

### 12.5 Arrival in China: cleared, held, or rejected — what each looks like

Three outcomes await at the Chinese port. **Cleared** is the normal one. The buyer's customs broker lodges the import declaration, customs reviews documents, perhaps inspects and samples, duties are paid, and the container releases in days. You may hear nothing except "goods received". Silence is usually success.

**Held** means questions. A document mismatch, a random inspection, a lab test in progress, a port backlog. Held is common and survivable. Your jobs: respond to information requests within hours, not days; supply any missing document instantly from your shipment folder ([Section 7.5](#ch07-5)); and keep daily contact with the buyer's broker through your buyer. Storage and demurrage clocks run during holds, which is why fast answers are money.

**Rejected** means customs has ruled the goods non-compliant: a failed residue test, live insects, or fatal document defects. Rejection triggers the four-option playbook of [Chapter 14](#ch14): re-export, redirect, destroy, or negotiate a resolution. What you must not do is panic-agree to the first cost split proposed in a stressed phone call.

Track every arrival to its end state, even the smooth ones. Ask the buyer how long clearance took and whether inspectors sampled. Each arrival teaches you the port's rhythm for the next one.

<a id="ch12-6"></a>

### 12.6 After delivery: feedback, relationship, reorder

The container is cleared and paid. Amateurs celebrate and move on. Professionals now execute the most profitable step in the whole cycle: converting one delivery into a relationship. Repeat orders cost almost nothing to win compared with finding a new buyer, and repeat buyers forgive small problems that would end a first deal.

Start with structured feedback. Ask the buyer three questions: How did the beans perform in use, including germination if sprouted? Were documents and packing exactly right? What would make the next shipment better? Write the answers into your shipment file and your product spec.

Close the loop backward too. Tell the packer how their beans performed in China; packers rarely hear end-user feedback, and they remember traders who provide it. Thank the forwarder and note what to adjust.

Then propose the reorder while success is warm: same spec, next shipment window, perhaps a modest volume step-up. 💰 **Example.** Sam's buyer took 20 tonnes and sold through in six weeks. Sam proposes a repeat container every eight weeks. Three repeat containers a year at Sam's AU$2,500 toy margin is AU$7,500 from one relationship, against one-off effort already spent. That arithmetic is why this section exists.

- [ ] My forwarder ships agri-containers to China and files my EDN.
- [ ] Loading day was photographed, sealed, and, ideally, independently witnessed.

## Chapter 13 — Getting Paid Without Getting Burned

<a id="ch13-1"></a>

### 13.1 The beginner-safe ladder: deposit up front, balance against documents

Payment safety is a ladder, and beginners belong on the lowest-risk rungs. The beginner-safe pattern has two locks. Lock one: a **deposit** before you commit money to beans and freight. The deposit proves the buyer is real and funded, and it shares the pain if the buyer walks away mid-deal.

Lock two: **balance against documents**. The buyer pays the remaining amount when you present the shipping documents, and you release the original bill of lading only after full payment. Remember the mechanism from [Section 3.4](#ch03-4): the original bill of lading is the ticket to collect the goods. Money before ticket, always.

💰 **Example.** Sam's AU$27,500 deal: AU$8,250 deposit at signing, AU$19,250 against document copies, originals released on receipt. Walk through Sam's worst cases. Buyer vanishes before shipment: Sam keeps the deposit and still owns the beans in Australia. Buyer vanishes after shipment: Sam holds the original documents and can redirect the cargo to another buyer ([Section 14.2](#ch14-2)). Painful, not fatal.

As trust builds over repeat orders, terms can slowly relax: smaller deposits, faster releases. Let the relationship earn each rung. Never start on a high rung because a stranger asked nicely.

<a id="ch13-2"></a>

### 13.2 Letters of credit demystified

A **letter of credit** (L/C) replaces trust in the buyer with trust in a bank. The buyer's bank issues a formal promise: present these exact documents by this date, and the bank pays. The buyer cannot cancel it unilaterally once issued. For sellers, this is the strongest standard protection in trade.

The machinery in five steps. One: the contract specifies payment by L/C. Two: the buyer's bank issues the L/C, listing required documents, amounts, and deadlines. Three: you ship and gather exactly those documents. Four: your bank presents them. Five: the issuing bank checks and pays.

The trap is the word "exactly". Banks pay against documents, not against goods or intentions. A **discrepancy**, any mismatch between documents and L/C wording, gives the issuing bank the right to refuse or delay payment. Misspelled names, late presentation, or a weight differing by one bag can all count. Beginners lose L/C protection through sloppy paperwork far more often than through bank failure.

Practical rules: keep the required document list short and achievable; have your bank check the draft L/C before the buyer issues it; and consider a **confirmed L/C**, where an Australian bank adds its own payment promise on top. L/Cs cost fees on both sides, so they suit larger or newer relationships. Many small pulse deals run happily on the deposit-plus-documents pattern instead.

<a id="ch13-3"></a>

### 13.3 Currency basics: AUD, USD, RMB — protecting your margin from FX swings

Three currencies circle your deal. Your costs are in Australian dollars (AUD). World commodity trade often prices in US dollars (USD). Your buyer thinks in Chinese yuan, called renminbi (RMB). **FX**, foreign exchange, is the conversion between them, and FX rates move every minute.

Why care? Because FX can eat a thin margin silently. 💰 **Example.** Sam signs a contract priced at US$10,000 for part-payment, when US$1 buys AU$1.50, so he expects AU$15,000. Payment arrives six weeks later, and the rate is now AU$1.40. His US$10,000 converts to AU$14,000. The move cost AU$1,000 without anyone breaking the contract. Against a AU$2,500 container margin, that is 40% of profit, gone.

Your defences, simplest first. Defence one: price in AUD when you can, pushing FX risk to the buyer; some buyers accept, some refuse. Defence two: shorten the exposure window with deposits and prompt payment terms. Defence three: hold USD in a foreign currency account ([Section 7.3](#ch07-3)) and convert when rates suit you.

Defence four: a **forward contract**, an agreement with a bank or FX provider that locks today's rate for a payment arriving later. Locking removes upside too; that is the price of certainty. RMB itself rarely reaches you directly; buyers convert through China's controlled system. Ask your FX provider about current options each year, because tools evolve.

<a id="ch13-4"></a>

### 13.4 ⚠️ When payment stalls: the escalation playbook

⚠️ One day a balance will not arrive on time. Run this ladder in order, and keep your tone professional at every rung; anger burns options.

Rung one, days one to three: the friendly nudge. Assume administrative friction, not theft. A polite message to your contact: "Our bank shows no receipt; can you check the transfer status?" Many stalls are genuinely bank compliance queues or a manager's travel.

Rung two, week one: the formal notice. A written email referencing the contract clause, the amount, the due date, and a clear new deadline. Copy the decision-maker, not just your friendly contact. Attach the invoice again. In parallel, have your Chinese ally make one soft phone call to read the real situation: cash problem, quality complaint brewing, or games ([Section 19.1](#ch19-1)).

Rung three, week two: leverage. If you still hold original documents, say plainly that they remain held and the cargo cannot release, and demurrage accrues to the buyer's account. If goods are released and this is a repeat relationship, freeze further shipments and quotes.

Rung four: endgame options. A negotiated payment plan with dates in writing. A formal demand letter from a lawyer. Trade credit insurance claim if you bought cover ([Section 7.2](#ch07-2)). Arbitration as contracted, weighed against cost ([Section 8.4](#ch08-4)). And always: report the experience honestly if industry peers ask. Reputation is the trade's oldest enforcement system.

- [ ] I can run an L/C with a short document list and a bank-checked draft.
- [ ] I know my FX exposure window and a defence for it, priced before signing.
- [ ] I follow the four-rung escalation ladder, professional at every step.

# PART IV — PITFALLS, RISKS, AND WAR STORIES

<a id="ch14"></a>

## Chapter 14 — The Rejection Playbook

<a id="ch14-1"></a>

### 14.1 Why cargo gets rejected: residues, insects, documents, labels

Rejection at a Chinese port has four classic causes, in rough order of pain. Cause one: **residues**. A lab result above a Chinese MRL, with glyphosate the famous killer for Australian pulses ([Section 1.4](#ch01-4)). Residue rejections are the worst because no negotiation fixes the number.

Cause two: **pests**. Live insects found at inspection, or fumigation paperwork that does not satisfy the officers. Grain insects breed during a warm voyage, which is why proper fumigation and clean containers matter ([Section 12.3](#ch12-3)).

Cause three: **documents**. A missing phytosanitary certificate, a facility registration number that does not match the CIFER database, an expired permit on the buyer's side, or inconsistencies across the pack. Document failures are the most preventable and among the most common.

Cause four: **labels and packaging**. Bag markings that do not meet requirements, or a retail-style product without compliant Chinese labelling ([Section 6.4](#ch06-4)). Notice something: every cause was covered by a prevention section earlier in this book. Rejection is almost never bad luck. It is a skipped step, surfacing at the most expensive possible location.

<a id="ch14-2"></a>

### 14.2 Your four options when it happens: re-export, redirect, destroy, negotiate

When rejection lands, you have four moves. Move one: **re-export home**. Ship the container back to Australia. You pay return freight, and the beans return to a market that already had them. This move suits high-value cargo with a fixable issue, and it is rare for beans.

Move two: **redirect**. Ship the container onward to a buyer in another country whose rules the cargo does meet. A residue level illegal in China may be legal elsewhere, because MRLs differ by country. Redirect usually beats re-export on freight cost and salvage price. This is where a network beyond China becomes survival equipment ([Section 19.3](#ch19-3)).

Move three: **destroy**. Authorities supervise destruction of the cargo, and you pay for it. Destruction is the last resort when redirect economics fail or authorities require it.

Move four: **negotiate**. Some holds short of formal final rejection can resolve: re-inspection, document correction, treatment at port, or downgraded use. Your buyer's broker and guanxi work this channel; a capable buyer here is worth their weight in beans. Decide with a spreadsheet, not with pride: salvage value minus each option's cost, within the deadline the port gives you. Deadlines are short, and storage charges run while you think.

<a id="ch14-3"></a>

### 14.3 Who pays the bill: insurance vs contract vs you

Now the ugly question: whose money burns? Start with insurance, and lower your hopes. Marine cargo insurance covers physical loss and damage in transit: water, fire, sinking, theft. A rejection because your beans carry too much glyphosate is not physical damage; standard policies do not pay for it ([Section 7.2](#ch07-2)). Specialised rejection insurance exists for some trades, at a price; ask a broker, and read exclusions twice.

Next, the contract. Well-drafted terms allocate this exact risk in advance. Key clauses: which country's MRLs define compliance; whose lab result is final; where risk passes under the Incoterm; and what happens on government rejection. If your contract says quality is final at loading per an independent lab against agreed specs, a later Chinese failure may sit with the buyer. If the contract promises "compliant with Chinese regulations at destination", it sits with you. Words you signed months earlier decide this day ([Section 8.2](#ch08-2)).

And when contract and insurance both leave gaps, the bill lands on you, the party who could not shift it. That is the default ending for the unprepared beginner.

So flip the logic while it is cheap. Before signing: read the rejection clauses, price the risk, and test the actual lot so the clause never wakes up. The cheapest payer of a rejection bill is the shipment that never gets rejected.

<a id="ch14-4"></a>

### 14.4 The prevention system: test twice, ship once

Everything in this chapter compresses into one system with four gates. Gate one: **source clean**. Buy from dual-registered packers, prefer mechanically finished crops, and demand the lot's residue certificate before purchase ([Section 10.4](#ch10-4)).

Gate two: **test the export lot**. The pre-shipment panel on the blended, bagged reality, against Chinese limits, at an accredited lab ([Section 10.5](#ch10-5)). This is the "test twice" in the motto: once at buying, once at shipping.

Gate three: **paper discipline**. The document pack complete and internally consistent, registration numbers verified against databases, buyer permits sighted, drafts checked by the buyer before issue ([Section 12.4](#ch12-4)).

Gate four: **rehearse the bad day**. Before each shipment, spend ten minutes on the pre-mortem: if this container is held, who do I call first, where is my document folder, what is my redirect market? A trader with a rehearsed plan negotiates a hold calmly on day one. A trader without one loses a week to shock, and pays that week in demurrage.

💰 **Example.** Sam's prevention stack per container: about AU$500 in tests plus AU$300 in inspection and document care, under AU$1,000 total. It defends AU$25,000 of capital. No insurance product sold anywhere offers that ratio.

## Chapter 15 — Scams, Fraud, and Bad Actors

<a id="ch15-1"></a>

### 15.1 Classic China-trade scams and their early warning signs

Most scams targeting new exporters are old plays with fresh paint. Learn the patterns and the warning lights. Pattern one: the **eager giant**. A "buyer" appears from nowhere wanting huge volume, agreeing to your price instantly, rushing to contract. Real buyers negotiate specs and test small first. Speed plus size plus zero questions equals warning.

Pattern two: the **victory trip**. The deal requires you to fly to China to sign, where you are treated to banquets, then pressed for "notary fees", "gift money", or contract charges before signing. Legitimate buyers do not charge sellers fees to accept their goods. The trip's purpose is your wallet, not your beans.

Pattern three: the **switched account**. Mid-deal, an email arrives: "Our bank account has changed; send the deposit here instead." The email looks like your counterparty's, but a fraudster has intercepted or spoofed the thread. Any bank-detail change demands voice verification on a number you already had, every time, no exceptions.

Pattern four: the **quality ambush**. Goods arrive, and suddenly a claimed defect demands a huge discount, with the cargo held hostage by time and storage fees. Defence: independent loading inspection and a contract that names the final lab ([Section 8.2](#ch08-2)). Across all patterns, one meta-rule: urgency is the scammer's oxygen. Slow every rushed decision by 48 hours, and most scams die quietly.

<a id="ch15-2"></a>

### 15.2 Fake buyers, fake documents, surprise "port fees"

Fake buyers are cheap to manufacture: a website, a borrowed registration number, stolen photos of warehouses. Your antidote is the four-layer verification of [Section 11.2](#ch11-2), plus one habit: verify independently. Do not call the phone number on the documents they sent; find the company's registered contact through the official registry or through your Chinese friends on the ground. A fake collapses the moment someone knocks on the real door.

Fake documents flow the other way too. Fraudsters posing as sellers or agents circulate forged bills of lading, forged phytosanitary certificates, and forged lab reports. When you buy or co-broker, verify documents at their source: the shipping line can confirm a bill of lading, DAFF can confirm a certificate, and a lab can confirm its own report number. Every issuer answers such queries daily.

Then the small-money classic: surprise **"port fees"**. After documents are sent, a message demands a few hundred dollars for an unexpected "release fee", "quarantine surcharge", or "documentation stamp", payable to a personal account, urgently. The amounts are sized to be paid without thinking. Real port charges appear on proper invoices from named companies through your forwarder or buyer's broker, never through a personal transfer to unblock a container.

The economics of fraud favour you if you use them. Every check above costs minutes. The scam costs everything. Spend the minutes.

If a scam does land, act within hours, not days. Tell your bank immediately; transfers can sometimes be frozen mid-route, and speed decides. Report to Scamwatch, the Australian consumer watchdog's scam service, and to police. Then warn your network: your packer, your broker, your industry contacts. Recovery of sent money is rare, so treat every report as protection for the next trader, and every warning you receive as protection for you.

<a id="ch15-3"></a>

### 15.3 Protecting your samples, brand name, and trademarks in China

Your intellectual property needs protection before it needs lawyers. Start with samples. Samples announce your quality to the market, and they can also be used against you: shown to your packer's other customers to squeeze prices, or passed to competitors as a sourcing map. Send samples with spec sheets marked with your company name, keep records of who received what, and treat sample requests from unverifiable companies with polite caution.

Now the big one: your brand name. China runs a **first-to-file** trademark system. Whoever registers a name first in China owns it there, regardless of who used it first elsewhere. A distributor, a rival, or a professional trademark squatter can register your brand name or its Chinese version, then legally block your own goods or sell it back to you at ransom prices. This has happened to famous Australian and global brands.

The defence is early and cheap relative to ransom. If you intend to build any brand ([Section 17.4](#ch17-4)), file Chinese trademark applications early: your English name, your chosen Chinese name, and your logo, in the right product classes. Choose your own Chinese brand name deliberately with native-speaker help, or the market will choose one for you, and someone else will register it.

Use professional help: a China-experienced trademark attorney, or IP Australia's guidance and Austrade's China IP resources as starting points. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: ipaustralia.gov.au and austrade.gov.au.

## Chapter 16 — Market and Political Risk

<a id="ch16-1"></a>

### 16.1 Price volatility and the seasonal cycle

Mung bean prices swing hard. The crop is small compared with wheat or soybeans, so one drought, one flood, or one big buying wave moves the market sharply. **Volatility** means the size and speed of price swings. Small crops mean high volatility, and mung beans are a small crop.

The swings have a seasonal skeleton. Australian harvest, roughly December to May, brings supply and usually softer prices. Chinese demand peaks pull the other way: pre-summer stocking for mung bean soup season, pre-Mid-Autumn mooncake production, and pre-New Year factory stocking ([Section 2.3](#ch02-3)). Around that skeleton, weather and policy add noise.

What does a small trader do with volatility? First, respect it: never hold unsold stock casually, because a 15% market drop can erase six containers of planned margin. Match purchases to sales; the safest first-container pattern is back-to-back, buying beans only after the sale contract and deposit exist.

Second, use the calendar: plan shipments into demand peaks, not after them. Third, keep records of every quote you receive, building your own price history. After two seasons, your notebook becomes a market instinct no report can sell you.

<a id="ch16-2"></a>

### 16.2 China policy risk: lessons from barley, wine, and lobster

Between 2020 and 2024, Australian exporters learned a brutal lesson in **policy risk**: the risk that government decisions, not markets, cut off your trade. During a diplomatic dispute, China imposed heavy tariffs on Australian barley and wine and effectively blocked Australian lobster, coal, and other goods. Billions of dollars of trade stopped, sometimes within weeks. Most measures were later lifted as relations warmed, but exporters who depended wholly on China had already bled for years.

The lessons are not "avoid China". China remains the biggest prize in the pulse trade. The lessons are about posture. Lesson one: political weather is real weather. Read trade news headlines about the Australia–China relationship as part of your job, not as background noise.

Lesson two: speed matters more than prediction. Nobody reliably predicts policy; survivors were the exporters who could redirect volume to other markets within months. Barley growers shifted to Saudi Arabia and elsewhere; wine sat in warehouses because wine had fewer alternative buyers. Be barley, not wine.

Lesson three: relationships on the ground give early signals. Importers often sense tightening customs behaviour before any announcement. A buyer who trusts you will share those signals ([Section 11.5](#ch11-5)). Diversification, the structural answer, gets its own section: [Section 16.4](#ch16-4).

<a id="ch16-3"></a>

### 16.3 Competing origins undercutting you — and how to answer

Some season, a competitor origin will undercut your price badly. Myanmar sells below Australian cost most years; a bumper crop there, or a currency slide, widens the gap further. Central Asian suppliers push into the middle market. Your buyer will forward you their quotes with a message like "market is much cheaper now". This is a test, and panic discounting is the failing answer.

Answer one: sell the difference, with evidence. Australian beans compete on residue integrity, germination performance, and counter-season freshness ([Section 2.2](#ch02-2)). For a sprout factory, a germination failure or a residue scare in their own market costs them more than your premium. Quantify it in their terms, in their currency, with your lab certificates as exhibits.

Answer two: know your walk-away number and hold it. Your cost-up arithmetic ([Section 11.4](#ch11-4)) already defined the price below which the deal loses money. Matching Myanmar prices with Australian costs is not competing; it is subsidising your buyer with your savings.

Answer three: segment. When the cheap wave floods the sprout-bulk market, premium niches hold value longer: certified low-residue lots, organic, specific varieties buyers ask for by name ([Chapter 17](#ch17)). And when a season truly belongs to cheap origins, the professional move is fewer containers, kept relationships, and patience. Markets rotate. Suppliers who survive the down season own the up season.

<a id="ch16-4"></a>

### 16.4 Diversification as survival: never one buyer, never one market

Every risk in this part ends at the same exit: diversification. **Diversification** means spreading your business across several buyers, markets, and suppliers so no single failure can kill you. For a trader, it is not a growth strategy. It is a survival requirement, and the war stories above are its evidence file.

Run the concentration test on your own plan yearly. What percentage of revenue depends on your largest buyer? On China as a whole? On one packer? A useful discipline as you grow: keep any single buyer under about half your volume, keep a second market genuinely active rather than theoretical, and keep two packers qualified ([Section 6.3](#ch06-3)).

💰 **Example.** Sam at year three ships six containers a year. Concentrated version: six to one Guangzhou buyer; one policy shock or one lost relationship cuts revenue to zero. Diversified version: three to Guangzhou, two to a Vietnamese noodle maker, one to a Middle East pulse importer ([Section 19.3](#ch19-3)). The same six containers, but the worst single failure now costs half, not all.

Diversification costs something real: extra relationship-building, extra market rules to learn, sometimes thinner margins on secondary markets. Pay it anyway, and pay it before trouble, because diversifying during a crisis means selling from weakness. The second buyer you develop in good times is the one who saves you in bad times.

# PART V — SCALING UP: From One Container to a Business

<a id="ch17"></a>

## Chapter 17 — From Trader to Trusted Brand

<a id="ch17-1"></a>

### 17.1 The certified low-residue premium play

The first scaling move costs no machinery: turn your compliance discipline into a product. Every container you ship already passes the four-gate prevention system ([Section 14.4](#ch14-4)). Most competitors' containers do not. Package that difference as a named offer: certified low-residue mung beans, every lot tested at an accredited lab against Chinese MRLs, certificates supplied with every shipment.

The buyer's logic makes the premium rational. A sprout factory that fails a residue check in its own market faces recalls, lost customers, and regulator attention. Your certificate stack converts their tail risk into your routine cost. Risk transferred honestly is worth paying for; that is the entire insurance industry in one sentence.

💰 **Example.** Sam's testing and documentation discipline costs about AU$40 per tonne in toy numbers (AU$800 on a 20-tonne container). He markets the certified program and prices AU$50 per tonne above uncertified equivalents. The buyer accepts, because a AU$50 premium is small against their failure costs. Sam's margin gains AU$200 per container while his risk drops. Compliance became revenue.

The play scales with consistency, not volume. One failed certificate ends the story, so the program must be real: every lot, every time, no shortcuts under schedule pressure. Discipline is the product. The certificates are just its packaging.

<a id="ch17-2"></a>

### 17.2 Locking in supply: grower agreements and contract farming

Scale changes your biggest fear. The beginner fears finding buyers. The growing trader fears supplying them. When you promise a buyer six containers across a season, a drought, a rival's big order, or a packer's capacity squeeze can leave you contract-bound with no beans. **Supply security** now deserves the effort you once spent on demand.

The first tool is the forward supply agreement with packers: contracted volumes across the season at fixed or formula prices, instead of spot purchases. A **spot purchase** buys whatever is available today at today's price. Forward agreements trade some flexibility for certainty on both sides, and packers welcome serious committed volume.

The deeper tool is **contract farming**: agreements made with growers before planting. You commit to buy defined tonnage of a defined variety at an agreed price or formula; the grower commits to grow it to your protocol, including the finishing practices that protect residue status ([Section 18.3](#ch18-3)). Some agreements add quality premiums for hitting germination or size targets.

Contract farming carries real obligations: you must take the crop that meets spec even when markets fall. Price that risk honestly, start with one or two trusted growers through your packer's network, and use GTA-style written terms ([Section 8.1](#ch08-1)). Controlled supply is what lets you sign the multi-container deals that build a real business.

<a id="ch17-3"></a>

### 17.3 The premium ladders: sprouting-grade and organic

Two ladders rise above the standard market. Ladder one: **sprouting-grade**, beans that sprout factories accept, defined by high germination, size, and cleanliness ([Section 1.3](#ch01-3)). You have met this ladder; climbing it means consistently supplying its top rungs: the highest germination lots, fresh-harvest timing, and varieties buyers request by name. Top-rung sprouting stock earns the strongest routine premiums in the trade, and Australian freshness in the counter-season window is a structural advantage ([Section 2.2](#ch02-2)).

Ladder two: **organic**, produced without synthetic chemicals under a certification system. Organic is not a marketing word; it is a legal-audit trail from paddock to bag. The grower, the packer, and even you as a handler may need certification from an approved certifier, and China additionally requires its own organic certification for goods sold as organic inside China. Dual certification is paperwork-heavy and slow to arrange.

The organic reward is a substantially higher price and access to health-focused buyers; the risk is a thin, specialised supply base and severe consequences if any link in the chain breaks certification. Enter organic only with a certified packer partner and a buyer contract in hand, not on speculation.

Climb one ladder at a time. Master certified sprouting-grade first; its systems, labs, and buyer conversations train you for organic's stricter version of the same game. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official sources: agriculture.gov.au for organic export rules and your certifier for China's organic requirements.

<a id="ch17-4"></a>

### 17.4 Building a name Chinese buyers ask for

A brand in commodity trade means one thing: buyers ask for your name when they could buy the same grade elsewhere. That preference is built from repetition, not advertising. Every on-spec container, every accurate document pack, every honest phone call when something slips: each is a brick. Chinese trade culture compounds this through reputation networks; wholesalers talk, and WeChat groups carry names fast, in both directions ([Section 11.3](#ch11-3)).

Give the reputation something to attach to. A simple, memorable trading name, a clean logo on your documents and bag marks, a professional bilingual spec sheet, and a chosen Chinese name for your business, selected with native-speaker care. Then protect the name: China's first-to-file trademark system rewards whoever registers first, so file early, in English and Chinese, before the name is worth stealing ([Section 15.3](#ch15-3)).

Make the name mean something specific. "Sam's beans clear customs without drama" is a brand. "Sam's certificates are always real" is a brand. Pick the claim your system genuinely delivers, and repeat it in every interaction until buyers repeat it to each other.

💰 **Example.** By year four, two Guangzhou wholesalers Sam has never met contact him, referred by his first buyer. Acquisition cost: zero dollars and four years of kept promises. That is what a name is: the discount rate on trust, paid forward.

## Chapter 18 — Owning More of the Chain

<a id="ch18-1"></a>

### 18.1 When to register your own facility (DAFF and CIFER)

So far you have rented compliance through registered packers, and rightly. At some volume, the arithmetic starts whispering: packing fees on every tonne, capacity queues at harvest peak, and your premium programs depending on someone else's machinery. Owning or leasing your own facility, registered with DAFF ([Section 5.2](#ch05-2)) and registered through CIFER for China ([Section 6.2](#ch06-2)), converts those fees into your own margin and your own control.

Respect what the move really costs. Registration itself is applications, audits, and standards compliance: hygiene, pest control, record systems, trained staff. The heavier load is everything around it: buildings, cleaning and grading equipment, working capital for stock, insurance, and the management burden of running a food facility to audit standard, permanently. A failed audit now stops your whole business, not just one supplier option.

A sane threshold test: registration talk begins when your annual volume approaches the scale of a small packer's throughput, when packing fees exceed what facility financing would cost, and when you have an operations person who can own compliance daily. For most traders that is many dozens of containers per year, not five.

There is an intermediate rung: leasing time in an existing registered facility, or a joint venture with a packer wanting export volume. Climb rungs, not cliffs. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official sources: agriculture.gov.au and cifer.singlewindow.cn.

<a id="ch18-2"></a>

### 18.2 Packing, grading, and other value-adding steps

**Value-adding** means transforming a product so buyers pay more than the transformation costs. The bean chain offers a menu, and you do not need a facility to start choosing from it; you can buy some steps as services before you own machinery.

Step one: precision grading. Optical sorters photograph each bean and eject discoloured or defective ones, lifting a Processing-grade lot toward premium spec. Where the grade price gap exceeds sorting cost, the sorter prints money. Step two: packaging formats. Bulk one-tonne bags suit factories; 25-kilogram bags suit wholesalers; small retail pouches serve supermarkets but drag you into full Chinese retail-labelling compliance ([Section 6.4](#ch06-4)). Climb toward retail only with a partner who owns that compliance.

Step three: splitting and processing. Split mung beans, flour, and starch serve different buyers at different margins, but each processing step adds machinery, food-safety obligations, and new competitors. Step four, often the highest return per dollar: information value-adding. Lot traceability, full certificate stacks, and consistent branded documentation cost little and directly serve your premium programs ([Section 17.1](#ch17-1)).

💰 **Example.** Sam trials optical sorting as a paid service. Sorting costs AU$30 per tonne and lifts his lot's sale price by AU$50 per tonne: AU$400 extra margin on a 20-tonne container, no machinery owned. Choose value-adding steps exactly like that: measured, reversible, service-first.

<a id="ch18-3"></a>

### 18.3 Solving glyphosate at the source: mechanical swathing services as a side business

Recall the root of the residue problem ([Section 1.4](#ch01-4)): mung beans ripen unevenly, so some growers spray glyphosate to dry the crop for one clean harvest pass. The chemical-free alternative is **swathing**: a machine cuts the crop and lays it in rows, called windrows, where it dries naturally before a harvester picks it up. **Desiccation** is the general term for drying a crop before harvest; swathing does it mechanically instead of chemically.

Why do not all growers swath? Equipment. Swathers are specialised machines, costly to own for one crop's short window, and contractors with the right gear are scarce in some districts at exactly the weeks everyone needs them. Growers spray because spraying is available. That gap is a business.

The model: a swathing service that contracts with growers in your supply network to cut their mung beans mechanically, priced per hectare, scheduled across the district's harvest window. You might own a machine, lease one, or partner with an existing agricultural contractor and bring them guaranteed bookings. Your trading arm then contracts those growers' glyphosate-free crops at a premium ([Section 17.2](#ch17-2)), which you sell through your certified program ([Section 17.1](#ch17-1)).

Notice the shape: the service solves the growers' harvest problem, secures your clean supply, and feeds your premium brand, three returns from one asset. Run the numbers with real contractor costs and honest utilisation estimates before buying any machine; agricultural equipment punishes optimism. Start with partnership, scale with proof.

💰 **Example.** In toy numbers, Sam partners with a contractor who owns a swather. Sam guarantees 500 hectares of bookings across his grower network at AU$60 per hectare, AU$30,000 of work. The contractor pays Sam AU$5 per hectare for the guaranteed schedule: AU$2,500. The growers get chemical-free harvests. Sam gets first call on their clean beans and a small service income. Nobody bought a machine, and everyone's risk stayed small.

- [ ] I trial value-adding steps as paid services before owning machinery.
- [ ] I understand swathing as the chemical-free answer to glyphosate.

## Chapter 19 — Leveraging Your Three-Country Network

<a id="ch19-1"></a>

### 19.1 The China side: friends → agents → a real distribution partner

Your China network should evolve through three deliberate stages. Stage one, **friends**, you know from [Section 11.1](#ch11-1): introductions, translation, reputation checks, market gossip. Friends are scouts. Scouts win battles but do not hold territory.

Stage two is the **agent**: a person or small company formally representing you in China under a written agreement, paid by commission or retainer. A good agent visits buyers, attends fairs under your name, chases slow payments in person and in Mandarin ([Section 13.4](#ch13-4)), and feeds you market intelligence weekly. The agreement defines territory, duties, commission, confidentiality, and exit terms. A friend can become the agent; the paperwork still gets signed ([Section 11.1](#ch11-1)).

Stage three is the **distribution partner**: a Chinese company that buys your volume on its own account and resells through its network, holding stock, financing customers, and defending your brand in-market. This is a deep commitment: they gain exclusive or semi-exclusive rights, you gain scale and lose direct customer contact. Choose one the way you would choose a business co-owner, because commercially, you are.

Sequence matters more than speed. Friends vet the agent. The agent's years of intelligence identify which company deserves partnership. Each stage builds the verification machinery for the next. Skipping stages means marrying a stranger.

<a id="ch19-2"></a>

### 19.2 The Iran side: what's realistic (trade knowledge, contacts, diaspora demand) and the hard limits (sanctions, banking) — why legal advice comes first

Iran sits deep in your family story, and ماش sits deep in Iranian kitchens. Be clear-eyed about what this asset is and is not. The realistic value is threefold. First, trade knowledge: Iranian pulse traders carry generations of skill in quality assessment, negotiation, and bazaar-style relationship trading; family conversations are a free masterclass. Second, contacts and credibility across the Persian-speaking commercial world, which extends far beyond Iran itself. Third, **diaspora** demand: Persian communities in Australia, Canada, the Gulf, Europe, and the United States buy familiar staples, including ماش, through legal import channels in those countries ([Section 19.3](#ch19-3)).

⚠️ Now the hard limits, stated plainly. Iran is subject to extensive international **sanctions**: legal prohibitions on many kinds of trade, payments, and dealings, imposed by Australia, the United States, and others. Australian law applies to you as an Australian resident, and United States sanctions reach much of the global banking system, which is why banks refuse most Iran-linked payments regardless of the goods. Penalties for violations are severe, including criminal liability. Sanctions rules are complex, change often, and depend on exact parties, goods, and payment paths.

So the rule for this book is absolute: obtain specialist sanctions legal advice before any Iran-related transaction, however small, however family-framed. Do not attempt structures whose purpose is to hide an Iranian connection from banks or regulators; disguised routing is itself a violation, and this book will not advise it. What a lawyer can do is map the lawful space: what is actually permitted, what requires a government permit, and what is simply closed.

The strategic conclusion is comfortable, though. Your Iranian advantage pays best where sanctions do not apply: knowledge, skills, and diaspora markets in third countries. Harvest the asset there. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source: DFAT's sanctions pages at dfat.gov.au, and consult a sanctions lawyer.

<a id="ch19-3"></a>

### 19.3 Beyond China: Vietnam, the Middle East, and Persian diaspora markets

Diversification ([Section 16.4](#ch16-4)) needs destinations. Three fit your profile. First, **Vietnam**. Vietnam imports mung beans steadily for its own sprout, dessert, and noodle traditions; its food factories value quality, and its ports are close to Australia's shipping lanes. Trade terms benefit from regional agreements between Australia and Southeast Asian countries. Vetting, contracts, and testing discipline transfer from your China playbook almost unchanged; the language and holidays differ, the game does not.

Second, the **Middle East**, especially the Gulf states. The region imports most of its food, hosts large South Asian and Persian communities that cook with mung beans daily, and pays reliably through conventional banking. Dubai functions as a re-export hub for the wider region: one buyer there can distribute your beans across several countries. Halal expectations, Ramadan demand cycles, and summer heat logistics are the local lessons to learn.

Third, the **Persian diaspora markets** your family network unlocks: Iranian grocers and food importers in Sydney, Melbourne, Toronto, Los Angeles, Hamburg, and the Gulf. These are lawful, bankable customers in ordinary jurisdictions, buying ماش and related pulses for communities that know exactly what good quality means. Volumes per buyer are smaller than a Chinese sprout factory's, but margins on trusted quality are strong, and your cultural fluency is a real moat.

💰 **Example.** Sam's year-five book: three containers to Guangzhou, two to Ho Chi Minh City, one split among diaspora grocers via a Dubai consolidator. One market's bad year now bends his business instead of breaking it.

✅ **Chapter 19 checklist**

- [ ] My China network is evolving in sequence: friends → written agent → distribution partner.
- [ ] I use the Iran asset for knowledge, contacts, and diaspora demand, never for sanctioned trade.
- [ ] A sanctions lawyer reviews anything Iran-adjacent before money moves.
- [ ] I am developing at least one non-China market in parallel with China growth.

## Chapter 20 — Systems, People, and Money at Scale

<a id="ch20-1"></a>

### 20.1 Trade finance: from savings to bank facilities

Growth eats cash. Every container locks up money from bean purchase until buyer payment, and six containers in motion can lock six times your first deal's capital. **Trade finance** is the family of borrowing tools that fund goods while they move, and graduating to it is how traders scale beyond savings.

The ladder starts informally: your own capital, then supplier terms, where a packer who trusts you allows payment days after delivery, effectively a free short loan. Next comes **invoice finance**: a lender advances most of an invoice's value once you ship, repaid when the buyer pays. Then a **trade finance facility**: a revolving bank line that funds purchases against confirmed sale contracts and shipping documents. Letters of credit help here too: an L/C from a strong buyer's bank ([Section 13.2](#ch13-2)) is collateral a lender can trust.

Lenders will study your record before they fund your ambition. They want clean financial statements, shipment files that prove a repeatable system ([Section 7.5](#ch07-5)), diversified buyers, and margins that survive interest costs. Your discipline from Parts II and III is literally your loan application.

💰 **Example.** Sam wants six containers in motion, needing AU$150,000 of working capital in toy numbers. Savings cover AU$50,000. An invoice-finance line advances 80% of each shipped invoice, covering the rest. Interest trims his margin per container, but volume triples. Financed growth is a trade: margin for scale. Price it deliberately, never desperately.

<a id="ch20-2"></a>

### 20.2 First hires and agents on the ground

Solo has a ceiling. One person cannot chase growers, drive documents, court buyers, and manage finance across time zones indefinitely; mistakes from fatigue cost more than salaries. Hire against your weakest critical function, not for status.

For a trading business, the first hire is usually an **operations coordinator**: the person who owns shipment execution, the 12-week countdowns ([Section 12.1](#ch12-1)), document packs, bookings, and lab schedules. Precision-lovers thrive here, and your documented systems ([Section 20.3](#ch20-3)) are their training manual. Your time then shifts to what only you can do: relationships and deals.

On-the-ground representation grows in parallel: the China agent from [Section 19.1](#ch19-1), perhaps later equivalents in Vietnam or Dubai ([Section 19.3](#ch19-3)). Pay structures matter: pure commission tempts agents toward any deal that closes; a modest retainer plus commission buys loyalty to your standards, not just to volume.

Every role, employee or agent, gets the same skeleton: a written agreement, defined duties, defined numbers, and a probation period with honest reviews. You learned this discipline with friends in [Section 11.1](#ch11-1); it applies to everyone who acts in your name. People carrying your name are carrying your brand ([Section 17.4](#ch17-4)).

<a id="ch20-3"></a>

### 20.3 Software and systems: CRM, documents, shipment tracking

Systems are how one trader's judgment becomes a company's habit. Three tools cover most of it. First, a **CRM**, customer relationship management software: the database of every buyer, supplier, agent, and prospect, with notes from every call and a reminder for every promised follow-up. Guanxi at scale is memory at scale; the CRM is that memory. Start simple, even a disciplined spreadsheet, then upgrade when volume demands.

Second, a document system. One cloud folder tree, one naming convention, one checklist per shipment, exactly as [Section 7.5](#ch07-5) began; at scale, add templates for every recurring document ([Appendix E](#appe) gives starters) and access controls so staff see what they need. The test remains speed under pressure: any document, any shipment, in two minutes.

Third, shipment tracking. Forwarders and shipping lines provide container tracking; assemble each active shipment's status, deadlines, and document state onto one board the whole team can see. The countdown checklist becomes software: tasks with owners and dates instead of memory and hope.

Resist the tool-collecting hobby. Every system earns its place by preventing a specific expensive error you have actually seen. Adopt one tool at a time, write the procedure it enforces, and train it. A mediocre tool used consistently beats an excellent tool used sometimes.

<a id="ch20-4"></a>

### 20.4 Milestones: the 5-container, 20-container, and 100-container businesses

Three milestones mark the road, each demanding a different you. The **5-container year** is the proven hobbyist-professional: one or two buyers, packer-sourced supply, savings-funded, solo-run on evenings and weekends. In Sam's toy arithmetic, five containers at AU$2,500 is AU$12,500 of margin: real proof, not yet a living. The milestone's test is repeatability: five clean cycles of the same system, documented ([Section 12.1](#ch12-1)).

The **20-container year** is the real business. Toy margin AU$50,000, before the premium programs that should be lifting it ([Section 17.1](#ch17-1)). This scale forces the graduations of this part: trade finance ([Section 20.1](#ch20-1)), a first hire ([Section 20.2](#ch20-2)), forward supply agreements ([Section 17.2](#ch17-2)), at least two markets ([Section 16.4](#ch16-4)), and systems that run without your daily heroics. Most traders who fail here fail at delegation, not at trade.

The **100-container year** is a trading company: staff in two countries, facility decisions on the table ([Section 18.1](#ch18-1)), bank facilities, contract-farmed supply, possibly your own brand pulled by buyers ([Section 17.4](#ch17-4)). Margins per tonne may be thinner than your boutique days; the business earns through volume, finance efficiency, and premium mix.

Do not worship the biggest number. Each milestone is a legitimate destination, and many excellent traders park deliberately at twenty containers of premium, well-paid work. Choose the milestone that fits the life you want, then build the system that milestone demands.

✅ **Chapter 20 checklist**

- [ ] I climb the finance ladder deliberately: savings, supplier terms, invoice finance, facilities.
- [ ] My first hire owns operations, freeing me for relationships and deals.
- [ ] My CRM, document system, and tracking board make my judgment a company habit.
- [ ] I have chosen my milestone — 5, 20, or 100 — and I am building what it demands.

## Appendix F — The 12-Month Starter Roadmap: From Zero to First Container

⚠️ A calendar for a committed part-time beginner, in Sam's toy world. Your speed will vary; the sequence should not.

**Months 1–2: Foundations.** Read Parts I–II. Join GTA and the AMA. Set up the company, ABN, GST registration, and bank accounts ([Chapter 7](#ch07)). Build the record-keeping system. Start watching prices weekly and reading trade news.

**Months 3–4: Level 0.** Order graded samples from two or three packers ([Section 9.1](#ch09-1)). Courier samples to Chinese contacts. Collect feedback on spec and price. Visit at least one packer and the Port of Brisbane. Shortlist dual-registered suppliers ([Section 6.3](#ch06-3)).

**Months 5–7: Buyer development.** Work all four hunting grounds ([Section 11.3](#ch11-3)). Verify every serious lead in four layers ([Section 11.2](#ch11-2)). If a strong buyer appears before you are container-ready, run Level 1: piggyback through an established exporter under a signed commission agreement ([Section 9.2](#ch09-2)).

**Months 8–9: Deal construction.** Negotiate spec and price from cost-up arithmetic ([Section 11.4](#ch11-4)). Sign the sale contract on safe payment terms ([Section 8.3](#ch08-3)). Receive the deposit. Contract the beans.

**Months 10–12: Execution.** Run the 12-week countdown ([Section 12.1](#ch12-1)): testing, booking, fumigation, loading, documents, sailing, arrival, balance payment. Debrief everything ([Section 12.6](#ch12-6)), thank everyone, and propose the reorder.

**Exit criteria for year one:** one clean container cycle completed, or a deliberate decision at the [Section 9.4](#ch09-4) tree that Level 1 remains your level. Both are wins. The only failing outcome is a skipped step.

## Appendix G — Staying Current: Where to Watch for Rule Changes (Because They Will Change)

Every rule cited in this book will change: decree numbers, MRLs, grant programs, tariff schedules, portal names. Treat this appendix as your update system, fifteen minutes per week.

**The watch list.** Subscribe or check monthly: DAFF industry notices and MICOR updates for China (agriculture.gov.au); GACC announcements via your buyer, packer, and broker networks (customs.gov.cn); DFAT for ChAFTA and sanctions changes (dfat.gov.au); GTA circulars for contract and rule updates (graintrade.org.au); AMA newsletters for industry and season news (mungbean.org.au); Austrade for grants and market alerts (austrade.gov.au).

**The human layer.** Rules often move in practice before they move on websites. Your packer hears DAFF changes first. Your buyer's customs broker feels GACC tightening first. Your forwarder sees freight and port changes first. Ask each of them one standing question every quarter: "What is changing?"

**The pre-shipment ritual.** Before every single shipment, re-verify four facts, even for repeat deals: the packer's registrations are current; China's MRLs for your residue panel are unchanged; the buyer's permits are current; and document requirements at the destination port are unchanged. Fifteen minutes against a AU$25,000 container.

**The book's promise, kept honest.** This book taught the shape of the system: the roles, the sequence, the risks, and the disciplines. Shapes age slowly; details age fast. When a detail here disagrees with an official source, the official source wins, always. ⚠️ Rules change. Check the official source — that sentence is the last rule of the book, and the only one that never will.
