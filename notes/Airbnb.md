# Airbnb

**The move: they went and knocked on doors themselves. And sold breakfast cereal to stay alive.**

## Day 0

2007. Two broke designers in San Francisco, Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia, can't make rent. A big design conference is in town and every hotel is full. So they blow up three air mattresses in their flat, throw up a crappy website called "Air Bed and Breakfast," and charge $80 a night.

Three strangers show up. That's it. That's the whole company on day 0 — three people sleeping on the floor.

## How they actually got people to know

**1. They chased events, not customers.**
They didn't advertise. They picked moments where a city physically ran out of beds. First that design conference. Then the 2008 Democratic Convention in Denver — 80,000 people descending on a city with 30,000 hotel rooms. Press wrote about the housing crunch, and Airbnb was the answer sitting right there.

**2. The cereal.**
They were dead broke and no investor would touch them. So during the 2008 election they made novelty cereal boxes — "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's." Hand-glued the boxes themselves, 1,000 of them, sold at $40 a box. Made about $30,000. That money kept the company breathing. It also got them on the news, which was really the point.

**3. They went to New York and knocked on every door.**
This is the real one. Their mentor Paul Graham told them: stop coding, go meet the users. So they flew to New York — where nearly all their listings were — and physically visited hosts. Sat in their kitchens. Asked what sucked.

They noticed the photos were garbage — dark phone pics of messy rooms. So they rented a $5,000 camera and went apartment to apartment shooting professional photos for free. Bookings in New York roughly doubled. There was no algorithm, no ad spend. It was two guys with a camera walking up stairs.

**4. The Craigslist hack.**
Everyone looking for a room was already on Craigslist. So Airbnb built a tool that let a host post their Airbnb listing straight onto Craigslist with one click — dragging Craigslist's audience back to them. They also emailed Craigslist landlords one by one saying "hey, you should list this on Airbnb too."

## Money, year by year

Airbnb was private until 2020, so the early numbers are press estimates, not audited books. Treat the first few as rough.

| Year | Revenue |
|---|---|
| 2008 | ~$0. The cereal boxes made ~$30k — that *was* the income |
| 2009 | tiny. January: $200/week. By March: $1,000/week |
| 2010 | ~$1M-ish |
| 2011 | ~$45M |
| 2013 | ~$250M |
| 2016 | ~$1.7B |
| 2019 | $4.8B |
| 2020 | $3.4B (Covid — bookings fell off a cliff) |
| 2024 | $11.1B |

## The lesson

Nothing they did on day 0 scaled. Cereal boxes. Personal visits. Taking photos yourself. Emailing strangers.

That was the point. Their line became famous inside startups: **"Do things that don't scale."** Get 100 people who genuinely love you before you worry about a million who've merely heard of you.

## Cost of day 0 marketing

Basically zero, plus a lot of shoe leather and a rented camera.

## Q&A

**Q: The Craigslist hack — is it legal to steal another website's customers?**

Customers aren't property. Competing for them is completely legal. The dodgy bit was *automating* the posting, which broke Craigslist's own rules. Craigslist has sued other companies for exactly that (3Taps, PadMapper). Airbnb never got sued — Craigslist just quietly closed the hole. So: grey area, against the rules, not criminal.

**Q: What was Craigslist's business model, and why did people leave it for Airbnb?**

Craigslist was free classified ads. It made money from only two things: job adverts, and estate agent listings in New York. Everything else was free.

Free meant ugly. No photos, no reviews, no profiles, no payment protection, and absolutely crawling with scammers. Airbnb offered those four missing things. That's the whole reason people moved.

**Q: The cereal boxes — you mean actual breakfast cereal?**

Yes. Actual cereal. 2008 US election. They printed limited-edition boxes, then hand-folded and hot-glued 1,000 of them in their own flat and filled them with cereal.

"Obama O's — the breakfast of change." "Cap'n McCain's — a maverick in every bite." $40 a box, sold as election collectibles.

Obama O's sold out — about 800 boxes, roughly $30,000. Cap'n McCain's flopped and they ate the leftovers for months.

**Q: Wasn't all this just luck? They didn't know they were inventing a business model.**

Mostly, yes — and that's the honest read.

A 2008 recession that made strangers willing to rent out a spare room, *and* travellers desperate enough for a cheap bed to sleep in one — they didn't engineer that. It landed on them.

But the luck landed on them because they were still standing when it arrived. Plenty of people got handed the same conditions and had already packed it in. Luck can't find you if you've quit.
