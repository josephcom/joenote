# Stock Market Strategies

Not financial advice — I'm not a licensed advisor. This is a map of how people *think*, so you can judge for yourself.

Your example is real and has a name: **asset-based investing**. Here's the full landscape, grouped by the underlying logic.

---

## 1. Asset plays (your example)

**The idea:** Buy a company for less than the break-up value of what it owns — land, buildings, cash, machinery, stakes in other listed companies. The assets are the "floor" under your feet.

**The traps:**
- **Stale or fake book value.** Land on the books at 1970s cost may be worth 50x today — great. But factories, ships and inventory are often worth *far less* than stated. Book value is an accounting number, not a price someone will pay.
- **The value trap.** The discount is real and it never closes. You can sit for 15 years being technically right and financially poor.
- **The asset isn't for sale.** A family-controlled firm sitting on prime land has zero intention of selling it. Without a catalyst, the value is locked behind glass.
- **Debt sits ahead of you.** Assets are gross; you own what's left *after* lenders, pensions, tax and legal claims. Heavily mortgaged land gives you nothing.
- **Cash burn.** If the operating business loses money every year, it eats the asset floor. The floor is sinking while you stand on it.

**How to spot the trap:** Read the notes to the accounts, not the headline. Check when assets were last revalued and by whom. Subtract *all* liabilities including pensions, leases and contingent legal claims. Ask: who controls the company, and have they ever returned cash to minority shareholders? Check free cash flow — is the floor rising or being eaten? And ask what the specific catalyst is (sale, spin-off, activist, new management, death of a founder). No catalyst, no thesis.

## 2. Classic value investing (cheap earnings)

**The idea:** Buy solid businesses trading at low multiples of profit or cash flow, on the bet the market is being too pessimistic.

**The traps:**
- **Cheap for a reason.** Coal, print media, mall retail, landline telecoms all looked cheap all the way down. A declining industry gets cheaper forever.
- **Peak-cycle earnings.** A commodity or housing stock looks cheapest at the *top* of its cycle, when profits are abnormally high and about to collapse.
- **One-off profits.** Selling a building inflates one year's earnings, making the P/E look tiny.

**How to spot it:** Look at 7–10 years of earnings, not one. Ask "is this business shrinking or just out of favour?" — check unit volumes and market share, not just profit. Strip out one-off gains. For cyclicals, invert the logic: a *high* P/E at the bottom of a cycle is often the safer entry.

## 3. Quality / compounder investing

**The idea:** Buy excellent businesses with durable advantages (brands, networks, switching costs) and hold for years. Pay a fair price, let compounding work.

**The traps:**
- **Overpaying for certainty.** Great company, terrible entry price. You can wait a decade just to get back to par.
- **Moats erode quietly.** Kodak, Nokia and Intel were all "obvious" quality once.
- **Story creep.** "Quality" becomes an excuse to never sell and never re-examine.

**How to spot it:** Compare today's valuation to its own 10-year range, not to hype. Watch return on capital — if it's trending down over 5 years, the moat is leaking. Watch whether the company is holding price without losing customers; that's the real moat test.

## 4. Growth investing

**The idea:** Buy fast-growing companies; earnings growth outruns a rich starting valuation.

**The traps:**
- **Growth is priced in.** The company can grow 30% and the stock still falls, because 40% was expected.
- **Unprofitable growth.** Revenue bought with discounts, ad spend or debt that never converts to cash.
- **Dilution.** Constant share issuance and stock-based pay means the *company* grows but your slice shrinks.
- **The law of large numbers.** Growth always decelerates. The de-rating when it does is brutal.

**How to spot it:** Track share count over 5 years — rising fast is a red flag. Look for cash flow, not "adjusted EBITDA". Ask what growth rate is baked into the price, and whether it's physically plausible (does it imply more customers than exist?). Watch for the first quarter of deceleration; that's usually the signal, not the recovery.

## 5. Dividend and income investing

**The idea:** Buy for the cash payout; get paid while you wait.

**The traps:**
- **The yield trap.** A very high yield usually means the market expects a cut. Yield is high because price fell.
- **Dividends paid from debt** or from selling assets, not from profit.
- **Ignoring total return.** A 7% dividend on a stock falling 10% a year is a slow loss.

**How to spot it:** Check the payout ratio against *free cash flow*, not earnings. Look at dividend history through the last recession — did they cut? Rising debt plus rising dividend is a warning. Be suspicious of any yield far above its sector's norm.

## 6. Index and dollar-cost averaging

**The idea:** Don't pick. Buy the whole market at regular intervals, cheaply.

**The traps:**
- **Concentration risk hiding in plain sight.** Many "diversified" indexes are dominated by a handful of giant stocks in one sector.
- **Home-country bias.** One national index can stagnate for decades (Japan 1990–2015).
- **Behavioural failure.** The strategy works only if you keep buying during crashes. Most people stop exactly then.
- **Fees and tax drag** on the wrong product.

**How to spot it:** Check the top-10 weightings of any index fund. Diversify across countries. Automate contributions so panic can't intervene. Compare total expense ratios.

## 7. Momentum / trend following

**The idea:** Buy what's already rising; winners tend to keep winning for a while.

**The traps:**
- **Violent reversals.** Momentum crashes hardest and fastest when it turns.
- **You're late.** By the time a trend is obvious, you're buying from the people who need an exit.
- **Overtrading.** Fees, spreads and tax quietly eat the edge.

**How to spot it:** Momentum without a pre-set exit rule is gambling. Define your stop-loss *before* buying. Beware anything up 5x in a year on no change in fundamentals.

---

## 8. Technical / chart trading

**The idea:** Price patterns and indicators reveal the next likely move.

**The traps:**
- **Pattern-hunting in noise.** Enough indicators will always confirm what you already want to do.
- **Backtest illusion.** Any rule can be tuned to look brilliant on past data.
- **Fees and taxes** on high frequency.

**How to spot it:** If a rule needs many parameters to work, it's fitted to the past. Test it on data you didn't design it on. Track your own results honestly — most self-reported chart records don't survive a spreadsheet.

---

## 9. Contrarian, turnaround and distressed

**The idea:** Buy the hated, the crashed, the near-bankrupt, and profit from the recovery.

**The traps:**
- **Catching a falling knife.** "Down 80%" can still fall another 80%.
- **Dilution rescue.** The company survives, but by issuing so many new shares that existing holders are wiped out anyway.
- **Averaging down** into a genuinely broken business — doubling your bet on your own mistake.

**How to spot it:** Focus on the balance sheet first. Can it survive two more bad years without raising money? Check debt maturity dates — that's the real deadline. Look for a *specific* fix (new CEO with a record, a divested loss-maker, a refinanced loan), not just "it's cheap now."

---

## 10. Special situations / event-driven

**The idea:** Profit from a defined corporate event — a merger, spin-off, buyback, rights issue, index inclusion, court ruling.

**The traps:**
- **Deal breaks.** Regulator blocks it, financing fails, and the stock snaps back to pre-deal price instantly.
- **Small upside, large downside.** You risk 30% to make 4%.
- **Information asymmetry.** You're trading against people who read the legal documents properly.

**How to spot it:** Read the actual deal terms and conditions precedent. Ask what the price is if the deal fails — that's your real risk. Regulatory or political objections are the most common killers.

---

## 11. Cloning: following insiders, gurus and tips

**The idea:** Copy company insiders buying their own shares, or big investors' disclosed holdings.

**The traps:**
- **Delayed data.** Fund disclosures are months old; they may have already sold.
- **You don't know the position size** or whether it's a hedge.
- **Insider buys can be cosmetic** — small purchases designed to signal confidence.
- **Pump-and-dump.** Social media "tips" often come from people already holding, who need buyers.

**How to spot it:** Prefer large, personal-money insider buys by operating executives (CEO/CFO) over token ones. Ignore option exercises. Never buy on a tip you can't independently justify. Ask: why is this person telling me?

---

## 12. Thematic and sector rotation

**The idea:** Pick the trend — AI, ageing, defence, green energy — and buy the sector.

**The traps:**
- **Right theme, wrong stock.** The internet was real; most 1999 internet companies still died.
- **Competition kills returns.** A booming sector attracts capital until nobody makes a profit (airlines, solar panels).
- **Buying the theme at the top,** usually when the themed ETF launches.

**How to spot it:** Ask who *captures* the profit in the value chain — often it's the picks-and-shovels supplier, not the glamorous end product. Watch capacity being added; that's the profit killer. New themed funds launching is usually a late-cycle signal.

---

## 13. Factor / quant investing

**The idea:** Buy baskets ranked by statistical traits — cheapness, quality, momentum, low volatility, small size.

**The traps:**
- **Factors underperform for a decade** at a stretch, long enough for you to quit at the bottom.
- **Crowding.** Once a factor is popular, its edge shrinks.
- **Data mining.** Hundreds of "factors" are published; most are noise.

**How to spot it:** Only use factors with long, cross-country evidence and a plausible reason for existing (risk or human behaviour). Assume the historical return will be lower in future.

---

## The traps that apply to everything

- **Confirmation bias.** Write down, in advance, what would prove you *wrong*. If you can't, you don't have a thesis.
- **Position size.** Most people are ruined by concentration, not by picking badly.
- **Leverage.** Borrowed money converts a temporary loss into a permanent one.
- **Illiquidity.** In small caps, getting in is easy, getting out is not.
- **Accounting quality.** Rising receivables or inventory faster than sales, frequent auditor changes, complex related-party transactions — these predict trouble across every strategy.
- **Governance.** A controlling shareholder who treats the company as personal property will destroy any thesis, however clever.
- **Your time horizon.** Nearly every trap above is really a mismatch between how long the strategy needs and how long you can actually wait.

The honest summary: no strategy is safe. Each one has a characteristic way of failing, and knowing *your* strategy's failure mode is more useful than knowing ten strategies badly.
