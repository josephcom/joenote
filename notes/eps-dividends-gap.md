# EPS-Dividends Gap

## 1. A loan has two parts

Bolt borrows 600 from a bank. The rate is 10% per year. So Bolt pays 60 of interest each year. This year Bolt also hands back 100 of the borrowed money. That 100 is the **principal**.

Interest is the rent on the money. Principal is the money itself.

## 2. Only one part is a cost

The income statement lists costs. Interest is a real cost, because Bolt pays for a service. So the 60 appears there.

The 100 is not a cost. Bolt only gives back money that it already got. Nothing is consumed. So the 100 never touches the income statement.

The 100 does two other things:
- The balance sheet shows the loan drop from 600 to 500.
- The cash flow statement shows 100 of cash leave the company.

This is why you saw "interest 60 + repayment 100" in one place, and only the 60 in the other place.

## 3. When is the principal due?

The contract decides. There are three common shapes:

- **Amortising loan:** Bolt pays a slice of principal every period, plus interest.
- **Bullet loan or bond:** Bolt pays interest only, then the whole 600 on the final date.
- **Partial amortisation:** Bolt pays small slices, then a big final payment.

A bullet loan creates refinancing risk. If Bolt cannot borrow again on the due date, then Bolt must find 600 in cash at once.

## 4. So EPS is not spendable money

Say Bolt earns a net profit of 200, and Bolt has 100 shares. The EPS is 2.

That 2 is not 2 of free cash per share. There are two reasons.

First, EPS ignores the principal. If Bolt must repay 100 this year, then a large part of the profit is already promised to the bank.

Second, EPS is not a cash number. It includes depreciation and accruals. So the profit and the cash in the bank are never the same figure.

## 5. Where the cash really goes

The order matters here. Two items come **before** free cash flow:

- **Capex**, which is money for new machines and buildings.
- **Working capital growth**, which is money stuck in stock and unpaid customer bills.

Bolt subtracts both from operating cash. What is left is **free cash flow**.

Then several needs fight over that free cash flow:
- the principal repayment,
- share buybacks,
- acquisitions,
- a cash buffer for bad years,
- and the dividend, which comes last in the queue.

So the dividend is often much smaller than the EPS.

## 6. Which statement answers the dividend question?

Go to the cash flow statement. The income statement cannot answer it, because the income statement never shows capex or principal.

But be careful here. The cash flow statement shows what Bolt **could** pay. It does not show what Bolt **chose** to pay, and it does not show why.

For the "why", you need two more sources. The first source is the management commentary in the annual report. The second source is the loan contract terms, which people call **covenants**.

## 7. The bank can limit the dividend

Loan contracts include **restricted payment clauses**. The lender caps the dividend so that the cash stays inside the company for repayment.

In plain speech, the bank says this:

> "Hey, I lent you 600. Do not hand my safety margin to your shareholders. So pay no dividend if your net debt goes above 3 times your EBITDA. Even below that, cap the dividend at half of your net profit. If you break this, then I want my money back."

## 8. Bolt must tell the public

Bolt is listed on a stock market. So Bolt must disclose material debt terms. This happens at two moments:

- **At signing.** A large loan triggers a prompt filing. The contract itself often comes attached as an exhibit.
- **After that.** The key terms repeat in the **debt note** in every annual and quarterly report.

## 9. Where the debt note sits

The debt note is not inside the income statement, the balance sheet or the cash flow statement. All notes sit after the three statements.

The statements give you the numbers. The notes give you the terms behind the numbers.
