# Stock Market Strategies

*A summary of our full conversation — every strategy, every question you raised, and the answers, arranged so the questions live under the strategy they belong to.*

**Not financial advice.** I'm not a licensed advisor. This is a map of how people think, so you can judge for yourself.

---

## 1. Asset plays — "a floor for the investor's feet"

**The idea:** Buy a company for less than the break-up value of what it owns — land, buildings, cash, machinery, stakes in other listed companies. This was your own opening example.

**The traps:**

- **Stale or fake book value.** Land carried at 1970s cost may be worth 50x today — great. But factories, ships and inventory are often worth *far less* than stated.
- **The value trap.** The discount is real and never closes. You sit for 15 years being technically right and financially poor.
- **The asset isn't for sale.** A family-controlled firm sitting on prime land has zero intention of selling it.
- **Debt sits ahead of you.** You own what's left *after* lenders, pensions, tax and legal claims.
- **Cash burn.** If the operating business loses money every year, the floor is sinking while you stand on it.

### Your challenge: "This can't go on for too long! Someone with a big pocket may come around at anytime, buy enough of the company and sell the property."

Right in theory. It does happen. But the arithmetic is harsher than it looks.

To grab the land you need **control**, which means paying a takeover premium (typically 20–40% above market), plus advisory and legal costs, plus capital gains tax when the asset is sold, plus stamp duty. So a 30% discount to asset value isn't an opportunity — it's roughly break-even. The discount has to be *huge*.

Then the blockers:

- **Control blocks.** If a family or founder holds 40%+, no hostile bid is possible. Ever. They simply say no.
- **The asset is load-bearing.** The factory sits on the land. Selling it means shutting the business.
- **Legal friction.** Heritage listings, zoning, long leases, environmental clean-up liabilities, foreign ownership rules.
- **Culture.** Hostile takeovers are rare in Japan, much of Europe, and family-heavy parts of Asia.

Japan proves both sides of your argument: thousands of companies traded below liquidation value for **thirty years**. It only began unwinding around 2023, when the stock exchange itself forced the issue. You were right, eventually. But "eventually" outlived a career.

**Test to apply:** who owns 20%+ of the shares, and can the asset be sold without killing the business? If a family controls it and the answer is no, walk away. No catalyst, no thesis.

## 2. Classic value investing — cheap earnings

**The idea:** Buy solid businesses at low multiples of profit or cash flow, betting the market is too pessimistic.

**The traps:**

- **Cheap for a reason.** Coal, print media, mall retail, landline telecoms all looked cheap all the way down.
- **Peak-cycle earnings.** A commodity or housing stock looks cheapest at the *top* of its cycle.
- **One-off profits.** Selling a building inflates one year's earnings, making the P/E look tiny.

**How to spot it:** Look at 7–10 years of earnings, not one. Ask "is this business shrinking or just out of favour?" Check unit volumes and market share. Strip out one-off gains.

### Your question: "What's cyclic about them? Every year? Or do they boom then decline until they're dead, like mines?"

Three different things get confused. Separate them:

- **Seasonal** — within one year. Retailers make most profit at Christmas. Predictable, priced in.
- **Cyclical** — multi-year boom and bust, repeating, **the industry never dies.** This is what I meant.
- **Secular decline** — structural death. Coal power, print newspapers, DVD rental. Down forever, no recovery.

**The engine behind a cycle is the lag between deciding to build and finishing.** Copper price spikes → every miner sees fat profits → they all approve new mines → but a mine takes 7 years to build → all that supply arrives at once, years later → glut → price collapses → everyone cancels projects → no new supply for years → shortage → price spikes. Repeat.

Typical cycle lengths: memory chips 2–4 years, shipping 7–10, mining and oil 7–15, housing 7–15, autos ~5.

**Your mine example is a separate thing.** An *individual* mine depletes and dies. The *industry* is cyclical. Don't confuse the asset with the sector.

**Examples of cyclical businesses:** miners (iron ore, copper, lithium, coal), oil & gas, shipping and dry bulk, memory semiconductors, airlines, homebuilders and construction materials, steel, base chemicals, autos, fertilisers, advertising, and banks (via the credit cycle).

**Why it matters:** at the peak, profits are enormous, so P/E looks tiny — "cheap!" — right before the crash. At the trough, profits are zero or negative, so P/E looks insane — right before the recovery. **For cyclicals, the P/E signal is inverted.**

## 3. Quality / compounder investing

**The idea:** Buy excellent businesses with durable advantages (brands, networks, switching costs) at a fair price and hold for years.

**The traps:**

- **Overpaying for certainty.** Great company, terrible entry price. You can wait a decade just to get back to par.
- **Moats erode quietly.** Kodak, Nokia and Intel were all "obvious" quality once.
- **Story creep.** "Quality" becomes an excuse to never sell and never re-examine.

**How to spot it:** Compare today's valuation to its own 10-year range. Watch return on capital — trending down over 5 years means the moat is leaking. Watch whether the company can hold price without losing customers; that's the real moat test.

## 4. Growth investing — and the dilution problem

**The idea:** Buy fast-growing companies; earnings growth outruns a rich starting valuation.

**The traps:**

- **Growth is priced in.** The company grows 30% and the stock still falls, because 40% was expected.
- **Unprofitable growth.** Revenue bought with discounts, ad spend or debt that never converts to cash.
- **Dilution.** Constant share issuance and stock-based pay.
- **The law of large numbers.** Growth always decelerates. The de-rating when it does is brutal.

### Your worry: "This takes good night sleeps away, because it's completely out of my control. Will I still have my shares tomorrow? If issuance changes the price, the whole market is a gambling casino!"

Three different things get lumped together as "dilution." Only one is theft.

**(a) Issuing at fair value.** $1,000 company, 100 shares, worth $10 each. It issues 10 new shares at $10 and receives $100 cash. Now $1,100 of value across 110 shares = **still $10 a share.** You own a smaller *percentage* of a bigger pie. Value per share unchanged.

**(b) Issuing at a discount (rights issues, placements).** Issues 10 new shares at $5, gets $50. Now $1,050 across 110 shares = **$9.55.** You lost 45c. But in a rights issue *you* get first refusal at $5 — take it up and you're square. Ignore it and you pay for it. This is why you read your shareholder mail.

**(c) Stock-based compensation.** Company hands 5 free shares to staff. $1,000 across 105 shares = **$9.52.** A straight transfer from your pocket to employees' pockets. It's salary paid with your equity, and companies love it because it doesn't hit cash flow.

### Your follow-up: "Even at fair value, the same earnings get divided between more mouths. It's a loss."

**This is the error.** It's *not* the same earnings. The company received cash.

$1,000 company, 100 shares, earns $100/yr → EPS = $1.
Issues 10 shares at $10, receives $100 cash.
If that $100 earns the same 10% return, earnings become $110 across 110 shares → **EPS = $1. Unchanged.**

So the real question is never "did they issue shares?" It's **"what return did they get on the money?"**

- Invested above the company's normal return → EPS *rises*. Dilution made you richer.
- Invested below → EPS falls. You were diluted for real.
- Issued below fair value, or given away as staff pay → straight transfer out of your pocket.

### And the part about your sleep

**Your shares don't move.** Nobody can take them, reduce them, or touch them. If you own 500 shares, tomorrow you own 500 shares. Dilution changes what *fraction of the company* those 500 represent — never the number.

**It isn't a surprise.** Issuance is announced publicly in advance. Large issues typically need shareholder approval — you vote. Rights issues give you first refusal, so you can maintain your exact percentage. Stock-based pay is disclosed annually, and the diluted share count is printed in the accounts.

**And it's already handled in your current strategy.** Index returns are *per-share* returns. Every dilution across ~1,500 global companies is already inside the VGS number you're happy with. You've been living with it comfortably the whole time — you just hadn't seen it named. So it's not "100% out of your control": it's disclosed, countable and predictable, which is the exact opposite of a casino.

**How to check it:** pull the diluted share count from the annual report for the last 10 years. Rising 1%/yr = normal. Rising 5%+/yr = you're funding someone else's salary. Falling = buybacks, and you're getting a bigger slice for free.

## 5. Dividend and income investing

**The idea:** Buy for the cash payout; get paid while you wait.

**The traps:** dividends paid from debt or asset sales rather than profit; ignoring total return (a 7% dividend on a stock falling 10% a year is a slow loss); and the yield trap.

### Your question: "Explain the yield trap more."

Dividend yield = dividend ÷ price. **The price is in the denominator.** So a collapsing price *manufactures* a beautiful yield.

Stock pays $1, trades at $20 → 5% yield. Bad news hits, price falls to $10 → the screen now says **10% yield.** Bargain! Except the market dropped the price *precisely because* it expects the dividend to be cut. Company cuts to $0.40 → your actual yield is 4%, and you're down 50% on capital.

**Checks:** Is the dividend covered by *free cash flow* (not accounting earnings)? Is debt rising while the dividend is maintained? Is the yield wildly above the sector average? Did they cut in the last recession? Any two of those = trap.

Also worth remembering: a dividend is your own money handed back to you, not free money.

## 6. Index investing and dollar-cost averaging — your current strategy

**The idea:** Don't pick. Buy the whole market at regular intervals, cheaply.

**The traps:** hidden concentration; home-country bias; behavioural failure (the strategy only works if you keep buying during crashes, and most people stop exactly then); fees and tax drag on the wrong product.

### Your question: "Home-country bias — didn't get this."

Simply: buying only your own country's index. Two problems.

**Concentration.** The ASX is roughly half banks and miners, with the top 10 names about 45% of it. That's not a diversified portfolio — it's a leveraged bet on Australian credit and Chinese steel demand.

**Single-country stagnation.** Japan's Nikkei peaked in 1989 and did not reclaim that level until 2024 — 34 years of "just wait, the market always recovers." Your line, *"the whole world is waiting just like me,"* is only true if you actually own the whole world. You've already fixed this by moving to a global fund.

### Your claim: "I already beat the market by switching from A200 to VGS. What can stop me from landing the next blow right in the face of the market?"

I won't tell you beating the market is impossible. But to be precise: **switching A200 → VGS was not beating the market. It was changing which market you own.**

That's asset allocation, and it was the right call — but it's an *ex post* result. Global equities beat Australian equities over that stretch; over 2000–2010 the opposite was true. You made a good diversification decision and got paid for it. That's different from generating alpha, and confusing the two is how smart people talk themselves into expensive experiments.

### Where you actually have a structural edge

1. **Time horizon.** Fund managers get fired for two bad years. You can't be fired. With your 10+ year horizon, this is your single biggest advantage — it's the only reason value and small-cap strategies remain uncrowded.
2. **Size.** You can buy a $200m company. A $50bn fund physically cannot.
3. **Complexity.** Spin-offs, rights issues, holding company structures, dual listings. Boring, fiddly, institutions skip them. You read financial documents — this is your lane.
4. **Costs and tax.** See section 12. Reliable percentage points a year, no forecasting skill required.

**Practical shape:** keep 90% in the index core. Run a 10% satellite. Write down every thesis *before* you buy, including what would prove you wrong. Benchmark it against VGS after tax and brokerage. Give it five years. If you're not ahead, you have your answer cheaply.

## 7. Momentum / trend following

**The idea:** Buy what's already rising; winners tend to keep winning for a while.

### Your objection: "When something moves up or down, there is 0 guarantee it doesn't reverse 1 second later."

You were right, and I had conflated two different things. Splitting them:

- **Discretionary trend-following** — a human eyeballing a chart, deciding it "looks strong." This is crap, exactly as you said.
- **Systematic momentum** — mechanically buying the top-decile 12-month performers and rebalancing monthly. This is one of the most replicated findings in finance: 200 years of data, every asset class, every country. It's real.

But real ≠ suitable for you. It's streaky, it crashes violently at market turns, and the high turnover is taxed at your full marginal rate (see section 12), which destroys most of the edge for an Australian long-term holder.

**Traps if you do it:** violent reversals; you're late, buying from the people who need an exit; overtrading fees. Never run it without a pre-set exit rule defined *before* you buy.

## 8. Technical / chart trading

**The idea:** Price patterns and indicators reveal the next likely move.

**Verdict: crap.** Pattern-hunting in noise — enough indicators will always confirm what you already wanted to do. Backtest illusion — any rule can be tuned to look brilliant on past data. If a rule needs many parameters to work, it's fitted to the past.

## 9. Contrarian, turnaround and distressed

**The idea:** Buy the hated, the crashed, the near-bankrupt, and profit from the recovery.

**The traps:**

- **Catching a falling knife.** "Down 80%" can still fall another 80%.
- **Dilution rescue.** The company survives, but by issuing so many new shares that existing holders are wiped out anyway.
- **Averaging down** into a genuinely broken business — doubling your bet on your own mistake.

**How to spot it:** Balance sheet first. Can it survive two more bad years without raising money? Check debt maturity dates — that's the real deadline. Look for a *specific* fix (new CEO with a record, a divested loss-maker, a refinanced loan), not just "it's cheap now."

## 10. Special situations / event-driven

**The idea:** Profit from a defined corporate event — a merger, spin-off, buyback, rights issue, index inclusion, court ruling.

**The traps:** deal breaks (regulator blocks it, financing fails, stock snaps back instantly); small upside for large downside (risking 30% to make 4%); trading against people who read the legal documents properly.

**How to spot it:** Read the actual deal terms and conditions precedent. Ask what the price is if the deal fails — that's your real risk.

### Your question: "Hang on! I thought big guys are big because they look at complex stuff as butter and bread!"

Your instinct is right about *ability* and wrong about *constraints*. They're not less capable. They're chained.

- **Size.** A $50bn fund wants positions of at least $250m. A $200m company can't absorb that. So the analysis isn't worth doing at any level of skill.
- **Mandate.** Many funds are legally restricted to index constituents, or to companies above a market-cap floor, or to one country. Not a judgment call — a rule in a document.
- **Forced selling.** The big one. When a company spins off a small subsidiary, index funds holding the parent **must dump** the spin-off, because it isn't in their index. They sell regardless of value, immediately, all at once. Your counterparty isn't wrong — they're *obligated*. That's the cleanest source of edge that exists.
- **Career risk.** Being wrong on something unusual gets you fired; being wrong on something conventional doesn't.
- **Research economics.** Same 40 hours of work whether you deploy $500m or $2m. Nobody covers the $2m version.

**Calibration:** the space isn't *empty*. Event-driven and activist hedge funds live there, and they're sharp. It's under-watched by the **largest** pools of money, not unwatched.

## 11. Small and micro caps

### Your question: "Where big players aren't looking, there might be big gold nuggets. Isn't this excellent?"

Yes — the logic is correct. Fewer eyes → more mispricing. That's genuinely where inefficiency lives. But the edge isn't free. What you're being paid for:

- **Illiquidity.** Buying is easy. Selling $50k of a thinly traded stock in a panic can move the price against you 10%+. Sometimes there's simply no buyer.
- **Wider spreads.** You lose 1–3% on entry and exit before anything happens.
- **Higher failure and fraud rates.** Weaker auditors, weaker boards, dominant founders. Some are outright frauds.
- **No safety net.** In large caps, 30 analysts have checked the numbers. In micro caps, *you* are the analyst.
- **Time.** Hours per company — reading annual reports, tracing related-party transactions, checking who owns what.

The nugget is real, but it's buried, and you dig it out yourself. Also note: the raw "small cap premium" weakened significantly after it was published in the 1980s. What survives is the *stock-picking* opportunity, not the category.

## 12. Cloning: insiders, gurus and tips

**The idea:** Copy company insiders buying their own shares, or big investors' disclosed holdings.

**The traps:** disclosures are months old (they may have already sold); you don't know position size or whether it's a hedge; insider buys can be cosmetic; and social media "tips" often come from people already holding, who need buyers.

**How to spot it:** Prefer large, personal-money insider buys by the CEO or CFO over token ones. Ignore option exercises. Never buy on a tip you can't independently justify. Ask: why is this person telling me?

## 13. Thematic and sector rotation

**The idea:** Pick the trend — AI, ageing, defence, green energy — and buy the sector.

**The traps:** right theme, wrong stock (the internet was real; most 1999 internet companies still died); competition kills returns (a booming sector attracts capital until nobody profits — airlines, solar panels); buying the theme at the top, usually when the themed ETF launches.

**How to spot it:** Ask who *captures* the profit in the value chain — often the picks-and-shovels supplier, not the glamorous end product. Watch capacity being added; that's the profit killer.

## 14. Factor / quant investing

### Your question: "I didn't understand this."

It's value investing turned into a spreadsheet, applied to 2,000 stocks instead of 5.

You rank every company in a market by a measurable number — cheapness (price ÷ book), profitability, debt level, past 12-month return, size, volatility. You buy the top 20%, ignore the rest, rebalance every 6–12 months. **No opinions, no stories, no CEO interviews.** The rule executes; you don't.

"Factors" are the traits that, across decades and dozens of countries, have historically explained returns. The main ones: value, quality/profitability, momentum, small size, low volatility. You can code this yourself, or just buy factor ETFs.

**The traps:** factors underperform for a decade at a stretch — long enough for you to quit at the bottom; crowding shrinks the edge once a factor is popular; and data mining means most published "factors" are noise. Only use ones with long, cross-country evidence and a plausible reason for existing.

Given your skill set, this is the most natural fit on the list.

## 15. Australian tax — the highest-certainty edge available to you

*General information, not personal advice. Rules change — worth an hour with a tax professional.*

**Franking credits (dividend imputation).** An Australian company pays 30% company tax, then attaches a credit to the dividend representing tax already paid. A 4% fully franked dividend is worth about **5.7% grossed up.** Australia is unusual in making these credits *refundable* — if your tax rate is below 30%, you get cash back. In a super fund in pension phase (0% tax), that's a full refund.

**Note the tension with your own move:** A200 carries franking credits. **VGS does not.** International shares pay foreign tax you can't reclaim. So the after-tax gap between them is narrower than the headline returns suggest — especially inside super. That doesn't make the diversification wrong, but the comparison you're using to declare victory is pre-tax.

**CGT discount.** Hold more than 12 months → individuals pay tax on only **50% of the gain.** Super funds pay on 33⅓%. Sell at 11 months and you pay full marginal rate, up to 47%.

This quietly kills most active strategies for you. A momentum system turning over every few months is taxed at full rate; your index holding is taxed at half, and only when you sell. **An active strategy has to beat the index by a large margin just to draw level after tax.**

**Super.** Contributions taxed at 15% instead of your marginal rate (up to 47%), earnings taxed at 15%, 0% in pension phase, within annual caps.

Add these up and you may be looking at **1–3% a year of extra return with no forecasting skill required.** It's arithmetic. It's the highest-certainty "market beat" on this entire list, and most people ignore it while hunting for stock ideas.

## 17. Day trading — your question about the 1–3%

Yes, you read it correctly: **roughly 1–3% of people who day trade are persistently profitable.**

**Taiwan** (Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean) — the entire market's day traders over multiple years. About 1% were *predictably* profitable, meaning past performance actually forecast future performance rather than being luck. Around 80% lost money.

**Brazil** (Chague, De-Losso, Giovannetti) — roughly 20,000 people who took up day trading equity futures. Of those who persisted more than 300 days, about **97% lost money.** Around 1% earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage. Roughly 0.5% earned more than a bank teller.

**Do the winners beat the market?** The top sliver do earn genuine abnormal returns — so yes, narrowly. Three caveats matter more than the headline:

1. **You can't identify yourself in advance.** The 1% is only visible looking backwards. Early profits do not predict later profits.
2. **The hourly rate is dismal.** These are full-time hours. Most of the profitable minority earn less than an ordinary salary.
3. **Taxed at full marginal rate.** No CGT discount. Your index holding is taxed at half. The day trader has to out-earn you by roughly double before you're even level.

Evidence also suggests the profitable minority skew toward people with professional infrastructure, speed advantages or prior institutional experience — not retail participants with a good brain and a laptop.

## 18. The universal traps (they apply to every strategy)

- **Confirmation bias.** Write down, in advance, what would prove you *wrong.* If you can't, you don't have a thesis.
- **Position size.** Most people are ruined by concentration, not by picking badly.
- **Leverage.** Borrowed money converts a temporary loss into a permanent one.
- **Illiquidity.** In small caps, getting in is easy; getting out is not.
- **Accounting quality.** Receivables or inventory rising faster than sales, frequent auditor changes, complex related-party transactions — these predict trouble everywhere.
- **Governance.** A controlling shareholder who treats the company as personal property will destroy any thesis, however clever.
- **Your time horizon.** Nearly every trap above is really a mismatch between how long the strategy needs and how long you can actually wait.

## 19. The bottom line you asked for

**Probability you can persistently beat the market — real edge, not luck, after tax and costs, over 10+ years: roughly 3–8%.**

The band is narrow because most of the range is decided by things I can't see: how much time you actually commit, whether you keep honest written records, whether you stay disciplined through a five-year drought, and whether you're willing to work in illiquid, boring corners rather than interesting ones.

*(If you count the tax and structure work — franking, CGT timing, super — as "beating the market," the probability is much higher, maybe 60%+. But that's arithmetic, not stock-picking edge.)*

**And if you land in that 3–8%, how much? Realistically 2–5% a year above VGS. So 12–15%, not 30%.**

- **+1–3%/yr** — most likely outcome. Unglamorous, but over 20 years it's a very large amount of money.
- **+3–5%/yr** — good. Rare. Where the best long-running small-cap and special-situations specialists land.
- **+5–8%/yr** — exceptional, career-defining, and usually involves concentration that can also go badly wrong.
- **+20%/yr sustained** — essentially doesn't exist over long periods.

For scale: Buffett's edge over the S&P was about 10 points a year across 60 years, and that made him the most famous investor alive. Peter Lynch ran roughly 13 points over 13 years and is a legend for it. Renaissance's Medallion fund is the extreme outlier — reportedly ~39% a year net — run by mathematicians and physicists with proprietary infrastructure, and closed to outside capital because the strategy doesn't scale.

**Two things cap you:**

**Compounding math.** 30% a year sustained turns $100k into ~$1.4m in 10 years and ~$20m in 20. If that were achievable by a numerate individual with a laptop, the strategy would attract capital until the edge vanished. Persistent high returns are self-destroying at scale.

**Tax and costs.** Your excess return is taxed at your marginal rate; VGS held long-term gets the CGT discount and only on sale. To clear 3% *after* tax you may need 5% before it.

**The uncomfortable implication:** 30% is the number that makes people take concentrated, leveraged bets — and leverage is the single most common way capable people permanently destroy a portfolio. **The return target itself is the risk.**

## 20. One-line summary

No strategy is safe; each has a characteristic way of failing, and knowing *your* strategy's failure mode is worth more than knowing ten strategies badly. Your edges are **structure, patience, tax and effort in ignored corners** — not speed, and not cleverness applied to the same information everyone else already has.
