# Cash & Debt Ratios
# The Ratios, Learned Through One Bakery

## 1. Meet the company

We use one company for the whole tutorial. It is **Bolt Bakery**. It bakes bread and sells it to supermarkets. All the numbers are in thousands, and they are round on purpose.

**What Bolt owns, and who paid for it:**

| Bolt owns (assets) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cash | 100 |
| Bread and flour in store | 100 |
| Money owed by supermarkets | 200 |
| Ovens, vans and the building | 600 |
| **Total** | **1,000** |

| Who paid for it | Amount |
|---|---|
| Bank loans (debt) | 600 |
| Owners (equity) | 400 |
| **Total** | **1,000** |

**What Bolt earned this year:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sales | 1,000 |
| Cash running costs | 800 |
| **EBITDA** | **200** |
| Wear and tear on ovens (depreciation) | 50 |
| **EBIT** | **150** |
| Interest on the loans (10% of 600) | 60 |
| Profit before tax | 90 |
| Tax (one third) | 30 |
| **Net profit** | **60** |

Two words to fix now. **EBITDA** is the profit before interest, tax and the charge for wear and tear. It is a rough guess at cash profit. **EBIT** is the profit after the wear and tear charge, but still before interest and tax.

## 2. Cash flow and free cash flow

Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. Here is the difference in Bolt's own numbers.

**Operating cash flow** is the cash that the daily business actually produced. Start at EBITDA and pay the real bills:

- EBITDA: 200
- Pay the interest: −60
- Pay the tax: −30
- **Operating cash flow: 110**

But Bolt cannot stop there. Ovens wear out. Vans die. Bolt must spend 60 this year to replace them and to add a little capacity. We call this spending **capital expenditure**, or **capex**.

**Free cash flow** is the cash that is truly free after all of that:

- Operating cash flow: 110
- Capex: −60
- **Free cash flow: 50**

That 50 is the only money that Bolt can use to repay debt, to pay a dividend, or to buy back shares. Hold on to that number. It is much smaller than the EBITDA of 200, and that gap causes most of the mistakes below.

## 3. Group A: how much debt is there?

**Net debt** = total debt − cash = 600 − 100 = **500**.

**Debt to equity** = 600 ÷ 400 = **1.5**. Lenders funded more of Bolt than the owners did.

**Net debt to EBITDA** = 500 ÷ 200 = **2.5**. This says that 2.5 years of cash profit would clear the debt. It is the number the market quotes most.

### What happens if you ignore it

Bolt's bank agreed a **covenant**. A covenant is a promise inside the loan contract. Bolt promised that net debt to EBITDA will stay below 3.5. Today it is 2.5, so there is room.

Now a supermarket drops Bolt and sales fall by 20%, from 1,000 to 800.

Most of Bolt's costs do not fall. Rent, wages and the ovens cost the same. Of the 800 of costs, 600 are fixed and 200 move with sales. So the costs fall only to 760.

- New EBITDA = 800 − 760 = **40**.

Look at what happened. Sales fell by 20%. EBITDA fell by 80%. Net debt to EBITDA jumped from 2.5 to about 13.

Bolt broke the covenant. The bank can now demand all 600 back at once. A poor sales year turned into a survival event, and it did so in twelve months.

**The lesson:** the ratio today is not the point. The point is how far the ratio moves in a bad year. If a company has high fixed costs, then a small sales fall causes a huge ratio jump.

## 4. Group B: can the company actually pay?

Group A measures the size of the debt. Group B measures the ability to service it. Companies do not fail because a ratio looks ugly. They fail because a payment falls due and the cash is not there.

**Interest cover** = EBIT ÷ interest = 150 ÷ 60 = **2.5**.

This means Bolt earns 2.5 times its interest bill. Above 5 is comfortable. Bolt is thin.

In the bad year, EBIT = 40 − 50 = **−10**. Interest cover is now negative. Bolt must pay 60 of interest out of a loss.

**Debt service cover** goes one step further. Interest is not the only payment. Bolt must also repay 100 of loan principal this year.

- Cash available before financing = EBITDA 200 − tax 30 = 170
- Total owed this year = interest 60 + repayment 100 = 160
- Debt service cover = 170 ÷ 160 = **1.06**

### What happens if you ignore it

This is the trap that catches most people. Interest cover of 2.5 looks weak but survivable. It hides the repayment. Once you add the 100 of principal, Bolt has almost nothing spare. A single bad month breaks it.

Now run the bad year through the cash:

- EBITDA 40, interest −60, tax 0 → operating cash flow = **−20**
- Bolt still must replace worn ovens, so capex is 50 → free cash flow = **−70**
- Cash was 100. It is now 30.
- The 100 repayment is due. Bolt has 30.

Bolt is profitable on paper in normal years, and it still cannot pay. **Always check the repayment, and not only the interest.**

**Free cash flow to net debt** = 50 ÷ 500 = **10%**.

Compare this with net debt to EBITDA. That ratio said 2.5 years to repay. Free cash flow says 10 years. Both are correct, and the second one is the truth. EBITDA ignores interest, tax and capex. Bolt pays all three in real money.

**The lesson:** if you judge debt by EBITDA alone, then you will believe a company can repay four times faster than it really can.

## 5. Group C: can it survive the next twelve months?

This group ignores profit and looks only at timing.

- **Current assets** (things that turn into cash within a year) = cash 100 + stock 100 + money owed by customers 200 = **400**
- **Current liabilities** (things Bolt must pay within a year) = supplier bills 100 + loan repayment 100 = **200**

**Current ratio** = 400 ÷ 200 = **2.0**. That looks safe.

**Quick ratio** removes the stock, because bread does not sell fast in a crisis:

- (400 − 100) ÷ 200 = 300 ÷ 200 = **1.5**

### What happens if you ignore it

A large supermarket customer goes bankrupt. It owes Bolt 150 and it never pays.

- Quick assets are now cash 100 + money owed 50 = **150**
- Quick ratio = 150 ÷ 200 = **0.75**

Bolt still holds 1,000 of assets on paper. It still cannot pay 200 next month. You cannot pay a supplier with an oven.

**The lesson:** liquidity is about timing, and not about total value. A company with plenty of assets can fail over a small bill.

## 6. Group D: does the debt create any value?

The three groups above ask whether the company survives. This group asks whether the company is worth owning.

**WACC** is the weighted average cost of capital. It is the blended price of all the money in the business.

- Debt costs 10%. Interest cuts the tax bill, so the real cost is about 7% after tax.
- Equity costs about 15%. Owners take more risk, so they demand more.
- Debt is 60% of the funding. Equity is 40%.
- WACC = (0.6 × 7%) + (0.4 × 15%) = 4.2% + 6.0% = **about 10%**

**ROIC** is the return on invested capital. It is what Bolt earns on every unit of money inside it.

- EBIT after tax = 150 × two thirds = 100
- Invested capital = 600 debt + 400 equity = 1,000
- ROIC = 100 ÷ 1,000 = **10%**

ROIC is 10% and WACC is 10%. Bolt earns exactly what its money costs. It creates no value at all.

### What happens if you ignore it

Management wants growth. It borrows 200 more and builds a second bakery. The new bakery earns 8%.

Profit goes up. Sales go up. The press release is happy. But 8% is below the 10% cost of capital. Bolt paid 10 for something that returns 8. Every unit of growth now destroys value.

**The lesson:** growing profit and creating value are different things. A company can grow itself into ruin by borrowing at 10% and investing at 8%.

## 7. The last trap: return on equity flatters debt

**Return on equity** = net profit ÷ equity = 60 ÷ 400 = **15%**.

Now build a twin. **Calm Bakery** is identical, with 1,000 of assets and EBIT of 150. It has no debt at all.

- Calm: EBIT 150, no interest, tax one third = 50, net profit = **100**
- Calm's return on equity = 100 ÷ 1,000 = **10%**

Bolt shows 15%. Calm shows 10%. Bolt looks like the better company. It is the same business. The only difference is the debt.

Now push both through the bad year, where EBIT is −10:

- Bolt: −10 EBIT, −60 interest = loss of 70 on equity of 400. That is **−17.5%**.
- Calm: −10 EBIT, no interest = loss of 10 on equity of 1,000. That is **−1%**.

**The lesson:** debt lifts return on equity in good years and destroys it in bad years. Never admire a high return on equity until you have checked the debt behind it.

## 8. The order to read them in

Each ratio protects you from one specific failure. Read them in this order.

1. **Net debt to EBITDA.** It tells you the size of the problem, and it tells you the covenant risk.
2. **Interest cover and debt service cover.** They tell you whether the company can pay this year. Debt service cover matters more.
3. **Free cash flow to net debt.** It tells you the truth after capex, and it corrects the EBITDA illusion.
4. **Quick ratio.** It tells you whether a single late payment can break the company.
5. **ROIC against WACC.** It tells you whether the whole exercise is worth anything.
6. **Return on equity, last.** Read it only after the five above, or it will mislead you.

Then run every one of them through a bad year, and not a normal one. Bolt looked acceptable on every measure until sales fell by 20%.
