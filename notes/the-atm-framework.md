# The ATM Framework

============================

**How much do I have to pay to buy "all" of this business? (A)**

> On a stock market, "all" costs more than the sticker price. Add a control premium, usually 20–40%. Use the diluted share count — options quietly grow A every year.

**How much "sellable" tangible stuff will it give to me? including cash reserves. (B)**

> "Sellable" means what someone would actually hand me for it today. Not what the balance sheet says it cost. Book value is the trap here.

**How much debt will it give to me? (C)**

> Not just bank loans. Leases, pension shortfalls, lawsuits, warranties, deferred tax, preferred shares. Anyone with a claim standing ahead of me.

**How much "fix" yearly cost will it give me? (D)**

> D doesn't go into the maths. It's my fragility gauge. If sales drop 30%, does D still get covered? If not, the business is brittle no matter how good J looks.

I am paying **E = A - B**

So, now my business owes **F = C + E**

Yes, my business ows me too. My business owes anyone who has poured money into it.

**How much money did come in? (G)**

**How much money did go out? (H)**

> H is everything out. Tax, all expenses, and the money spent just to keep the machines alive. If it left the bank, it's in H.

**I = G - H**

> Use a 3–5 year average. One good year is not I.
>
> If interest is sitting inside H, compare I to E, not F. Otherwise I'm counting the debt twice.

It will take **J = F/I** years for my business to become a risk free ATM.

> Not risk free — J is how long I'm exposed. The business can die in year 3.
>
> Sanity check: a government bond pays me back in ~25 years with no risk at all. So J of 20 isn't worth the trouble. J under 10 is where it gets interesting.
>
> And can I actually reach the cash? Money trapped in a subsidiary or another country isn't mine.

## Why B matters

Buy a lemonade stand for $1000. The stand and machines are worth $1000. This business owes me nothing. It's a free ATM from day one. Every dollar of profit I make, I can spend and enjoy right away, because I'm not worried about losing my initial investment. I can sell the stand at any time and my money is right there.

Now make the stand worth $600. I can't spend and enjoy my profit until I've saved up $400, so I know my initial investment is safe and intact.

B is my floor. The only catch: it has to be the **resale** $1000, not the receipt $1000.

## The standard terms

| Mine | Standard term | Where I find it | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Market capitalisation (price × diluted shares) | Not in the statements — market data. Share count in the equity note or cover page | Add control premium if buying 100% |
| **B** | Net tangible assets / tangible book value. Cash portion = Cash & equivalents | **Balance Sheet** | Carried at cost, not resale price. My whole argument rests on resale price |
| **C** | Total debt = short-term + long-term borrowings + lease liabilities | **Balance Sheet** (+ notes for leases, pensions) | Off-balance-sheet items hide in the notes |
| **D** | Fixed operating costs → operating leverage. Rent, salaries, SG&A, interest expense | **Income Statement (P&L)** + notes | The P&L doesn't split fixed from variable — I estimate it |
| **E** | Premium over tangible book (paid-for goodwill) | Derived | — |
| **F** | **Enterprise Value** (EV = market cap + debt − cash) | Derived | F is textbook EV |
| **G** | Cash receipts from customers / operating inflows | **Cash Flow Statement** (operating) | Revenue is the P&L cousin, not the same thing |
| **H** | Operating outflows + capital expenditure | **Cash Flow Statement** (operating + investing) | Capex sits in investing — easy to miss |
| **I** | **Free Cash Flow** (Operating cash flow − Capex) | Derived from Cash Flow Statement | — |
| **J** | **EV/FCF multiple** = payback years. Inverse = FCF yield | Derived | Cousin: EV/EBITDA |

---

## The real question

The arithmetic is the easy part. J tells me how many years I need the cash flow to hold up.

So: **will I survive J years?**
