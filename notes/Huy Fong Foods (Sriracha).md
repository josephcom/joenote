# Huy Fong Foods (Sriracha — the rooster sauce)

**The move: a bloke in a blue van, driving bottle by bottle to restaurants. And then — literally zero advertising. Ever.**

## Day 0

1980, Los Angeles. David Tran is a Vietnamese refugee who arrived on a freighter the year before with basically nothing. Back in Vietnam he'd made chilli sauce; in America he can't find a chilli sauce that tastes right to him.

So he starts making it himself. In buckets. In a small building in LA's Chinatown. He grinds fresh red jalapeños, no fancy equipment, family doing the work.

Nobody has heard of him. There is no marketing budget. There is no marketing *person*.

## How he actually got people to know

**1. He got in a van and drove.**
He bought a blue Chevy van and personally delivered the sauce, restaurant to restaurant, around LA. Pho shops. Chinese places. Vietnamese places. He'd walk in with a few bottles and ask them to try it.

That's the whole go-to-market. One man, one van, one restaurant at a time.

**2. He put it on the table, not on a billboard.**
This is the genius bit. Once a pho shop had it, the bottle sat on the table next to the soy sauce and the hoisin. Every single customer who sat down saw it. Picked it up. Used it. Read the rooster.

The restaurant table *was* the advertising. Free, permanent, and in front of thousands of people a week. Customers then went looking for it in shops.

**3. He made the bottle impossible to forget.**
Clear bottle so you see the red. Green cap. A rooster on the front — he was born in the year of the rooster. Most people didn't even know the brand name; they just said "the rooster sauce" or "the one with the green cap." That's a brand doing its job.

**4. He refused to raise the price.**
For decades he kept it cheap on purpose, even when he could easily have charged more. His view: sell a rich man's sauce at a poor man's price. Cheap meant restaurants kept stocking it, and stocking it kept the flywheel going.

## Money, year by year

Here's the honest answer: **we mostly don't know.** Huy Fong is a private family company that has never published accounts, never taken investment, and never had to tell anyone anything. Anyone quoting you a clean year-by-year table is guessing.

What is actually on the record:

| Year | Revenue |
|---|---|
| 1980, first month | ~$2,300 |
| 1980s–2000s | steady grind. No public figures at all |
| 2012 | ~$60M |
| 2013 | ~$85M |
| 2019 | ~$131M |
| Peak (approx) | ~$150M |

No debt, no investors, no marketing department, the whole way.

## The lesson

Huy Fong has famously **never spent a dollar on advertising** — no TV, no billboards, no agency, no social team, for over forty years. It grew into a sauce doing well over $100 million a year purely on word of mouth and restaurant tables.

His actual strategy was: put the product where people will *use* it in public, make it look unmistakable, keep it cheap, and let them do the talking.

## Cost of day 0 marketing

The petrol in a second-hand Chevy van.

## Q&A

**Q: Do we know what he made in year 1, year 2, and so on?**

Barely. First month of trading in 1980 was about **$2,300** — that's the one hard early number that exists, because Tran has repeated it in interviews.

After that, nothing, for roughly thirty years. He never raised money, so he never had to show anyone a set of books. The next reliable figure is around $60M in 2012.

The gap in the numbers is the story, really. He wasn't chasing a valuation or a funding round. There was nobody to report to. He just sold sauce.
