# The story of Spanx

**The move: she cold-called her way in, then did a live demo in a department store bathroom.**

## Day 0

Late 1990s. Sara Blakely is 27, selling fax machines door to door in Florida. Hates it. Getting doors slammed in her face all day.

She's got a party to go to, white trousers, and no underwear that doesn't show a line. So she grabs a pair of pantyhose, cuts the feet off, and wears that. Works great. That's the product. That's the whole invention — scissors and a pair of tights.

She had $5,000 saved. No fashion background, no investors, no factory, no contacts. She wrote her own patent application off a book from Barnes & Noble to save the lawyer fees.

## How she actually got people to know

**1. She rang the factories until one cracked.**
She drove to North Carolina, hit the hosiery mills, and got told no by every single one. Weeks later one owner rang back — he'd shown the idea to his daughters and they'd said "Dad, this is genius." That's her manufacturer.

**2. She cold-called the buyer at Neiman Marcus.**
No agent, no intro. Just picked up the phone and asked for 10 minutes.

**3. The bathroom demo.**
This is the moment. She flew to Dallas, sat down with the buyer, and could feel it going nowhere. So she said: come to the toilets with me. She put on white trousers with Spanx on, then without. Buyer sees the before and after with her own eyes, in about 30 seconds.

Spanx got into seven Neiman Marcus stores. No ad campaign. One woman, one pair of white trousers, one loo.

**4. She bought her own stock.**
Getting on the shelf isn't the same as selling. So she rang round her friends, gave them cash, and sent them into the stores to buy Spanx — so the numbers looked good and the buyer would reorder. She also turned up in stores herself and personally sold to shoppers, and moved her product from the dead back aisle to the counter by the till, using a display stand she built herself.

**5. Then Oprah.**
In 2000, Oprah named Spanx one of her Favourite Things. Phones melted. But note the order — Oprah came *after* she'd already hustled it into real stores and made it move.

## Money, year by year

| Year | Revenue |
|---|---|
| 1998–99 | £0. Just her $5,000 savings going out the door |
| 2000 (year 1) | ~$4M |
| 2001 (year 2) | ~$10M |
| 2002 (year 3) | ~$20M |
| 2012 | ~$250M — Blakely becomes a billionaire, still owning 100% |
| 2021 | ~$400M+. Blackstone buys a majority stake at a $1.2B valuation |

She never took a penny of outside investment before that Blackstone deal. Not one investor for 21 years.

## The lesson

She never bought an advert in the first years. Not one. Her marketing was: **get the product physically on a decision-maker's body, in the room, right now.**

Also — she never asked permission and never hired anyone to do the awkward bits for her. The bathroom trip is the entire strategy in one image.

## Cost of day 0 marketing

$5,000 total, and most of that was the patent and the product itself. Marketing spend: roughly nothing.

## Q&A

**Q: What is a patent, in plain words?**

A deal with the government. You publish exactly how your thing works; in return, for about 20 years, nobody may legally sell it. Only in the countries you file in. Costs real money. And it doesn't stop copycats by itself — you have to pay lawyers to chase them.

**Q: She went to manufacturers before she had any customers. Isn't that backwards? Why would a factory make something with no proven demand?**

She wasn't asking them to gamble. She was a paying customer buying a production run with her own $5,000. They said no because she was a nobody with a weird small order — not because of demand. One said yes. The risk was entirely hers.

**Q: What is Neiman Marcus?**

Posh American department store. Think Harrods — expensive, where rich women shop.

**Q: Who is "the buyer"? A person? A shop? A wholesaler?**

**A person.** An employee of the department store whose entire job is deciding which brands the store stocks. The gatekeeper.

One buyer says yes and you're on the shelf in every branch. If sales look weak, that same person doesn't reorder and you're dropped.

That's the key to the whole story. When Blakely gave her friends cash to go and buy Spanx, and when she moved her display to the till, she wasn't doing marketing. She was managing **one specific person's spreadsheet** — because that spreadsheet decided whether she existed next month.
