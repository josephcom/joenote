## Question 1: Occurrence 

Depending on who and what we are and what we already know, people put different labels on the same data. For example, we hear a sheep's call and we may put one of these labels on it: "noise" (soldier: "I can't sleep"), distress (farmer: "my sheep may be sick"), food (hungry man: "that sounds like milk!"), count/number (statistician: "oh, exactly 7 times! The other sheep was 5 times")

Then, we store it in our memory under that same label. Not only that, but, from that moment, everything else in our memory under the same label may pop up. Which one first? The loudest one — the one which has been repeated most often, or used most recently, or carries the strongest feeling.

One excellent habit is to control the labels we put on the input data. But the first label isn't really a choice: it fires on its own, before we notice. What we can control is what comes after it. So the real move is to add a second and third label to the same data. Keep asking "what else" is an excellent strategy.

## Question 2: Why can't we answer every question?

**The frame**

Treat a question as a machine with four parts:

**TARGET** — something definite to find. 
**SEARCH** — a route to it. 
**TEST** — a way to know you've arrived.
**RECEIVER** — a mind that can hold the answer once it's got it.

Break any one part and the question stays open forever. Four buckets. Everything fits.

Worth noticing before we start: the reasons people usually give — not enough information, too many possibilities, missing prerequisites — are *all* search failures. That's one bucket out of four, and the softest one.

------

### 1. TARGET — nothing there, or too many things

**No fact of the matter.** Some questions have no answer to find. Not hidden — absent. Future free choices. Counterfactuals: what if I'd married her. Self-referential loops, where Gödel proved that any system rich enough to do arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove *about itself*. Nothing is missing. There's no target.

**The question demands a sharp edge on a fuzzy thing.** Where does the forest end? Is a virus alive? When does a heap become a pile? Concepts are human tools with soft borders. Asking one for a hard boundary is asking a cloud for its perimeter. We brought the wrong instrument, then blamed the world.

**Every "why" is secretly several questions.** Try it. *Why did Adam eat the apple?* Now stress a different word. Why did **Adam** eat it — rather than Eve? Why did he **eat** it — rather than plant it? Why the **apple** — rather than the pear? Same sentence. Three questions. Three answers. Every why carries a hidden *rather than what?*, and if you haven't fixed the contrast, there is no single thing to find.

And here's where your Occurrence chapter arrives from an unexpected direction. **The label you slap on the input chooses the contrast — which means the label writes the question.** The soldier hears noise, so his question is "why loud rather than quiet." The farmer hears distress: "why sick rather than well." The statistician hears a count: "why seven rather than five." They are not answering one question with varying skill. They are answering three different questions, each correctly. Your labelling theory isn't only about what surfaces from memory. It's upstream of that. It builds the question before you've noticed you asked one.

------

### 2. SEARCH — the answer exists, we can't reach it

**No stopping rule.** Your chess case, and it's clean. "The best move" means we tried everything, scored it, took the top. But best at what depth? A move that's best at three plies is a blunder at thirty. Pick a depth and you've picked the answer. And who says the benchmark is the best benchmark — by what benchmark? These questions don't get answered. They get *dissolved* — you add the missing bounds and the question becomes answerable, and smaller.

**Missing input.** The answer exists, someone holds it, you don't. Your soldier with the recording on his phone. The cheapest failure in the whole list — one phone call — and permanent if the holder is dead or silent.

**Missing floor.** Your tower of knowledge, and the strongest thing in your notes. You cannot answer *why is ice slippery* without molecules; molecules need atoms, need chemistry, need centuries. Not one missing brick — a missing chain, and you can't see the shape of what's absent. Vocabulary counts too: with no word to hold a thought, the thought can't be held.

**Too big to search.** Everything is defined and available; you still can't get there. 10^120 chess positions. A pure resource wall.

**Retrieval failure.** You already own every piece and none surfaced. *Who said I have to go to work today?* This is your first question — the answer sat in memory under a label you never reached for.

**And the one that breaks the model:** some knowledge cannot be gathered into one place. Nobody knows how to make a pencil — the miner, the logger, the chemist each hold a fragment, and no head holds all of it. Worse: **you know more than you can say.** How do you ride a bike? You do it daily. You cannot state it. Some answers exist only in a form that doesn't convert into sentences. Not lost. *Unspeakable.*

------

### 3. TEST — the one nobody thinks about

Suppose God hands you a card with the true answer. Could you tell it from a very good wrong one?

Usually not. And this is fatal, because **search without a test is worthless.** Exhaustive search — your key concept #2 — quietly assumes a bell rings when you hit the answer. No bell rings.

Look what this does to your loop. You wrote: guess, hunt counterexamples, accept when none appear, stay open for the next one. The engine of that loop isn't the search. It's the test. So the sharper question isn't *why didn't a counterexample occur to me* — it's **where does my sense of "that's it" come from, and why do I trust it?**

You answered it yourself, almost in passing: *we stop as soon as we feel we've answered them all.* **Feel.** The stop signal is an emotion — the drop in tension when confusion lifts. That same drop arrives when you're bored, when the answer is merely familiar, when it's socially convenient, when you're tired. Your test is a comfort detector in a lab coat. Which is exactly why you also noticed we never ask *what else could explain all of this* — comfort has already fired, and the loop has no reason to run.

**And the test is often slower than the search.** You can generate fifty answers to "is this the right career?" in a minute. Verifying one takes forty years and destroys the alternatives in the process. Generation is cheap; verification is ruinous. That asymmetry alone rules out most questions that actually matter.

------

### 4. RECEIVER — the answer won't fit in a head

**Some things can't be compressed.** To explain is to compress: a good answer is smaller than the thing it explains. If the phenomenon has no shorter true description, no explanation exists — only the thing itself. Why did that neural net produce that output? The honest answer is four hundred billion numbers. There is no shorter true version. Un-answerable is wrong; it's un-*explainable*, permanently, at any level of intelligence.

**No instrument measures itself.** Your brain can't hold a model of your brain, because the model would have to contain the model. Every mind has exactly one blind spot and it sits precisely where the mind is standing. *Am I biased? Is my reasoning sound? What am I missing?* — structurally closed from the inside. This is the only bucket that doesn't yield to more time, more data, or more intelligence.

------

### The test for anything I've missed

Hand the question to a genie: infinite time, infinite information, perfect recall, no body.

If the genie answers it, your obstacle was only ever **expensive** — a search problem, bucket 2, the whole familiar list. If the genie is stuck too, you've hit a **target, test, or receiver** problem — and those are the real ones.

------

### What this costs your other ideas

Your **"pile up of knowledge"** has a leak. Tacit and incompressible answers don't stack — they can't be handed over, so each generation rediscovers them by doing. That's why we inherit calculus for free and relearn heartbreak from scratch.

And your ice: physicists still argue. Faraday proposed a thin liquid film in 1859. The pressure-melting story everyone repeats is wrong — a skater's weight isn't remotely enough. Premelting, frictional heating, both, weighted differently by temperature. **165 years in your loop and it hasn't closed.**

Which is the real answer to your question. The loop isn't a procedure that terminates. It's a condition you live in. What ends an inquiry is never *we found it* — it's **funding, death, boredom, or the feeling of having finished.**

## Dirty notes

Are we humans intelligent because we've been receiving experience from previous generation, add to it, and transfer it to the next one? If so, an "overlap" between generations is necessary to provide enough time for the transfer. It's a millions-year-old feedback **loop**.

Is intelligence nothing but *feedback loops*? If so, we're trapped and doomed! We have no choice but to *wait* until something happens for us to update our knowledge or *add another dot to the emerging **pattern***.

Even *neural networks* work based on feedback loops.

What are **concepts and ideas**? Are they just *multidimensional patterns*? If Einstein was blind and deaf since 2, then he would have remained as intelligent as a cat! So, *the more we observe, the more abstract our thinking becomes* and we become more independent? Is that it? *Intelligence=Abstract thinking*?

## Real cases of thinking process

### why is ice slippery?
Immediately something came to my mind (we call this **guess**), then I went into a loop to find any counterexample: if ... then why...? If ... then why ...? Once my theory answers all, then I accept it. 

- how and why the initial guess was formed? Why doesn't it **occur** to some other people? 
- we *temporarily* accept it until another counterexample shows up and we can't explain it. Then, we try to initiate another guess that, this time explains all the cases. Then, again, we *temporarily* accept it until another counterexample shows up and we can't explain it. And this can go forever. 
- how and why some examples and counterexamples do or do not occur to us? Why does it take so long, maybe forever (!) to **come up** work another counterexample?
- we stop as soon as we feel we've answered them all. We don't ask **what else** can answer them all.
